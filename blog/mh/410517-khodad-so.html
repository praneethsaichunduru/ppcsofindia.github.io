Giant Meter Wave Radio Telescope

जायंट मीटरवेव्ह रेडिओ दुर्बीण

The Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope takes its name directly from its function: an array of radio antennas purpose-built to observe the universe at metre wavelengths, a band largely neglected by earlier instruments elsewhere in the world. The site, near the village of Khodad roughly 80 kilometres north of Pune, was chosen only after the National Centre for Radio Astrophysics (NCRA) surveyed numerous locations across India for the combination of low man-made radio interference, firm ground suitable for anchoring heavy antenna foundations, and adequate access and infrastructure. No religious or legendary origin attaches to the facility; its founding story is instead one of deliberate scientific site-selection carried out through the 1980s by Indian astronomers seeking a home for a telescope that did not yet exist anywhere else at this scale.

As a research instrument, the GMRT occupies a singular place among the world's radio observatories: it is the largest and most sensitive array operating specifically at metre wavelengths, a regime useful for studying distant galaxies, pulsars, supernova remnants and the neutral hydrogen that traces the structure of the early universe. It is owned and operated by the NCRA, a semi-autonomous institute of the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (TIFR), and functions as a national and international user facility, with observing time allocated competitively to astronomers from India and abroad. Its scientific standing rests not on any single discovery but on decades of continuous service as one of the handful of instruments worldwide capable of this kind of low-frequency observation at high resolution.

Conceived and led by Govind Swarup, regarded as the founder of radio astronomy in India, the project moved from land acquisition in 1990 to construction beginning in 1991. The first of its thirty dish antennas was erected in 1993, all thirty were in place by 1995, and test astronomical observations began in 1996, with the array entering routine scientific operation by the early 2000s. Each antenna is a fully steerable parabolic dish 45 metres in diameter, and the thirty dishes are arranged in a roughly Y-shaped configuration spanning baselines of up to 25 kilometres, a layout that allows the array to be combined electronically into a single instrument with far greater resolving power than any one dish alone. Notably, the dishes use an indigenous low-cost "SMART" (stretch mesh attached to rope trusses) design developed in India specifically to keep construction costs down without sacrificing precision.

A defining episode in the telescope's history came with its systematic upgrade into the uGMRT, which replaced the original narrow-band receivers and analog signal paths with new wideband feeds, receivers and a modern digital backend. Formally inaugurated in March 2019 and opened to the global astronomical community that April, the upgrade increased the telescope's sensitivity by roughly a factor of three and gave it continuous frequency coverage across its observing bands rather than the narrow windows available before, substantially expanding the range of science the array can pursue, from pulsar timing to the hunt for fast radio bursts.

The facility today also supports dedicated high-performance computing projects, such as the SPOTLIGHT system built for real-time, round-the-clock searches for pulsars and fast radio bursts using GMRT data, and the uGMRT has since been used to detect low-frequency emission from repeating fast radio bursts at frequencies as low as 300 MHz. Administered throughout by the NCRA-TIFR, the observatory continues to draw visiting astronomers and collaborative international projects to the otherwise quiet farmland around Khodad, where the dish antennas remain a working research tool rather than a static monument.
