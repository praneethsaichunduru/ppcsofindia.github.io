Bibi Ka Maqbara

बीबी का मकबरा

Bibi Ka Maqbara owes its creation to a promise of remembrance rather than an ancient legend. Its subject, Dilras Banu Begum, the first and chief wife of the Mughal prince Aurangzeb, died in 1657 while giving birth to her youngest son, Muhammad Akbar, and was later honoured with the posthumous title Rabia-ul-Durrani. Having reportedly accompanied Aurangzeb on a visit to the newly completed Taj Mahal in Agra in 1652, she is said to have expressed admiration for it and a wish for something comparable for herself. Her son, Prince Azam Shah, fulfilled this wish by commissioning her tomb in Aurangabad roughly a decade after her death, giving the monument its colloquial name, meaning simply 'Tomb of the Lady'.

As a funerary monument, Bibi Ka Maqbara occupies a distinct place in Indo-Islamic architecture as the only grand Mughal-style mausoleum built in the Deccan plateau, far from the imperial heartland of Agra and Delhi. It is widely known by the epithet 'Taj of the Deccan' or 'Mini Taj Mahal' because of its direct visual debt to Shah Jahan's masterpiece, yet it stands independently as the primary surviving example of large-scale Mughal tomb architecture commissioned under Aurangzeb's reign, a period otherwise associated with a comparative decline in royal patronage of monumental building. Today it is designated a centrally protected monument of national importance, placing it alongside the country's foremost historical sites under statutory heritage protection.

Construction took place between approximately 1660 and 1661, and according to an inscription at the main entrance, the project was executed by the architect Ata-ullah, son of Ustad Ahmad Lahori who had been the principal designer of the Taj Mahal, working alongside the engineer Hanspat Rai. Facing a far more modest treasury allocation than the Taj Mahal, the builders used marble only up to the dado, or lower wall level, switching to basalt rubble finished with polished lime plaster for the upper walls and employing marble again for the dome. The tomb sits within a formal charbagh, a quadripartite Persian-style garden divided by water channels, and is raised on a plinth with four tapering minarets framing the central domed chamber, following the compositional template of its Agra predecessor at a visibly reduced scale.

A notable architectural choice distinguishes it from the Taj Mahal: rather than placing Dilras Banu Begum's actual grave at platform level beneath the dome, the builders set it in a hexagonal chamber reached by a flight of stairs below ground, encircled by a finely carved octagonal marble lattice screen, or jali, that admits a soft, dappled light onto the grave itself. This true burial chamber beneath the ornamented cenotaph above gives the interior a more austere, almost subterranean character than its famous model. The complex also includes a mosque to the west of the main enclosure, built later, which continues to serve as an active place of prayer alongside the tomb's function as a heritage site.

Maintained under the Archaeological Survey of India's Aurangabad circle, the monument has been the subject of periodic conservation work addressing plaster erosion, water seepage in the garden channels, and discoloration of the marble dome caused by decades of pollution and biological growth, with cleaning and restoration drives carried out in recent years to bring sections of the structure closer to their original finish. It remains among the most visited ticketed monuments in Maharashtra, drawing both domestic tourists and researchers of Deccan Mughal history, and continues to anchor Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar's identity as a city built around layered dynastic monuments rather than a single imperial centre.
