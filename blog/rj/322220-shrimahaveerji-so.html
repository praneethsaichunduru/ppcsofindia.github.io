Shri Mahaveer Mandir

श्री महावीर मंदिर

Shri Mahaveer Mandir, also widely known as the Shri Mahaveerji temple, stands at Chandanpur, a village on the bank of the Gambhir river in the Hindaun block of Karauli district, after which the surrounding settlement itself came to be renamed Shri Mahaveerji. Local tradition holds that the temple's principal idol lay buried and forgotten for centuries until a cowherd noticed one of his cows repeatedly emptying its milk over a particular patch of ground; when villagers investigated and dug at the spot, they unearthed a carved image of Mahavira, an event still recounted as the founding legend of the site and the reason the place is classed among the Digambara tradition's atishaya kshetras, or miracle-associated pilgrimage sites.

The temple is one of the most important Digambara Jain pilgrimage centres in northern India, dedicated to Mahavira, the twenty-fourth and final Tirthankara, whose teachings of non-violence, truth and renunciation underpin Jain doctrine. Its standing among atishaya kshetras distinguishes it from an ordinary place of worship, since such sites are believed to be marked by a miraculous event connected with a Tirthankara's image, and this status has made the temple a destination not only for Jains but also for Hindu devotees and, notably, for local Meena and Gujjar communities who join the annual festivities, giving the shrine a composite regional following beyond its core Jain constituency.

Following the idol's excavation, which accounts describe as having occurred roughly two centuries ago, a temple complex was raised over the find and subsequently expanded through patronage credited to figures associated with the Bilala Jain lineage, with the sanctum developed to enshrine the recovered image as its central object of worship. The complex that has grown around the original shrine today includes multiple associated Digambar Jain temples in the vicinity, among them the Panch Balyati temple and a Shantinath temple, reflecting the layered construction typical of Jain tirthas where subsidiary shrines accumulate around an original miraculous site over successive generations of donors and local Jain trusts.

The temple's defining annual event is the Mahavirji fair, held around Mahavir Jayanti in the Hindu month of Chaitra, when the five-day mela draws pilgrims in large numbers for worship, trade and a rath yatra in which the deity's image is drawn through the town on a bullock-pulled chariot. The scale of this gathering, which begins two days before Mahavir Jayanti and concludes two days after, turns the otherwise modest town of Shri Mahaveerji into one of the busiest pilgrimage and fair sites in eastern Rajasthan for that brief period each spring, with temporary markets and encampments forming around the temple precincts to accommodate the influx of visitors from across the region.

Access to the temple has been formalised through the Shri Mahaveerji railway station on the Delhi-Mumbai line of what is now the West Central Railway zone, a stop specifically developed to serve pilgrim traffic and located a short drive from the temple, with shuttle transport arranged by temple authorities to ferry visitors from the platform to the shrine. The site today functions under the management of a Digambar Jain trust that oversees its daily rituals, maintenance of the temple cluster, and the logistics of the Chaitra fair, and it remains, alongside the more celebrated Jain complexes of Dilwara and Ranakpur, one of the destinations that anchors Rajasthan's network of active Digambara pilgrimage sites.
