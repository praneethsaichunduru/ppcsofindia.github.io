Mehrangarh Fort

मेहरानगढ़ किल्लो

Mehrangarh takes its name from the Sanskrit "Mihir," meaning the sun god, and "garh," meaning fort, giving the structure its popular identity as the "Fort of the Sun," an epithet also tied to the Rathore clan's claimed solar lineage. According to local legend, Rao Jodha had to displace a hermit named Cheeria Nathji, who lived on the rocky hill of Bhaurcheeria, to clear the site for construction, and the holy man cursed the new fort with perpetual water scarcity. To placate him, Rao Jodha built the hermit a house and temple within the fort walls, and in a further act believed necessary to appease the site's spirits, a man named Raja Ram Meghwal was reportedly buried alive in the foundations, his family afterward granted land and upkeep in Raipur village that descendants still hold.

As the citadel of the Rathore rulers of Marwar, Mehrangarh ranks among the most significant Rajput forts in India, both for its scale and for the continuity of its use from the fifteenth century into the present as a museum and cultural institution rather than a ruin. It is frequently cited alongside Chittorgarh and Kumbhalgarh as one of Rajasthan's great fort complexes, and its hilltop dominance over Jodhpur has made it inseparable from the city's identity as the gateway to the Thar Desert. The fort's museum, developed from the royal collections, is widely regarded as one of the best-organised and best-curated fort museums in India, drawing large numbers of domestic and international visitors annually and contributing substantially to Jodhpur's tourism economy.

Construction began in 1459 under Rao Jodha on a ridge known as Bhaurcheeria, about 125 metres above the surrounding plain, and the fort subsequently grew over nearly five centuries through the contributions of rulers including Maharaja Jaswant Singh and Maharaja Ajit Singh, who added many of its most ornamented palace quarters. The fort is entered through a sequence of seven fortified gates, among them Jai Pol, built to commemorate victory over the armies of Jaipur and Bikaner, and Lohapol, the iron gate near which handprints of royal widows who committed sati are still visible on the stone. Within the walls, the Moti Mahal, Phool Mahal, Sheesh Mahal, and Sileh Khana display finely carved sandstone latticework, mirrored and gilded surfaces, and painted ceilings that reflect successive generations of Rajput craftsmanship rather than a single architectural campaign.

Among the fort's most striking features is the sheer scale of its ramparts, which rise up to 36 metres and, by some measurements, run as thick as 21 metres at their base, making the fortifications largely impervious to the cannon fire Mehrangarh withstood during its history, including an attack by Mughal forces in the seventeenth century that left visible cannonball marks on Jai Pol's surface. The Chamunda Mata temple, housing the Rathore dynasty's chosen deity and installed by Rao Jodha shortly after the fort's founding, sits at the fort's edge overlooking the city and was the site of a severe stampede during Dussehra festivities in September 2008, in which roughly 250 people died, prompting lasting changes to crowd management at the temple's approach.

Since 1972 the fort and its collections have been managed by the Mehrangarh Museum Trust, established by the former royal family under Maharaja Gaj Singh II, which oversees conservation, the display of royal weaponry, palanquins, textiles, and miniature paintings, and a range of restoration projects carried out in partnership with international conservation bodies. The trust has also developed the Rao Jodha Desert Rock Park on the fort's periphery, an ecological restoration of the native rocky scrubland surrounding the ramparts, extending the site's role beyond a historical monument into an active centre for heritage and environmental conservation in Jodhpur. The fort today functions as a ticketed heritage site and venue for the annual Rajasthan International Folk Festival, drawing musicians and visitors to its courtyards each October.
