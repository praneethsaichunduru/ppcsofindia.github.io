Maharana Pratap

महाराणा प्रताप

Maharana Pratap Singh I was born on 9 May 1540 at Kumbhalgarh Fort to Maharana Udai Singh II of Mewar and his queen Jaiwanta Bai. He ascended the throne of Mewar in 1572 following his father's death, inheriting a kingdom already pressed by the expanding Mughal empire under Akbar, who had absorbed most of the surrounding Rajput states into alliance or vassalage. Unlike several contemporary Rajput rulers who accepted Mughal overlordship through matrimonial and political alliances, Pratap refused any such accommodation, a stance that set the course for his entire reign and shaped his later reputation as Mewar's most uncompromising ruler.

As the thirteenth Rana of the Sisodia dynasty, Pratap holds a central place in Rajput history as the ruler who kept the house of Mewar formally independent of Mughal suzerainty throughout his lifetime, even as his territory shrank to the hill tracts of the Aravallis. His significance rests less on territorial conquest than on the symbolic weight of his defiance, which later Rajput chroniclers and nationalist historians of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries elevated into a narrative of principled resistance to imperial authority. He is commemorated today across Rajasthan and wider India through statues, place names, educational institutions, and state honours bearing his name.

The defining episode of his reign was the Battle of Haldighati on 18 June 1576, fought in a narrow mountain pass named for its turmeric-coloured soil in the Aravalli range near Khamnor. Pratap's force, estimated at a few thousand men, confronted a much larger Mughal army led by Raja Man Singh of Amber, reinforced by artillery and allied Rajput contingents; the terrain's narrowness blunted the Mughals' numerical and cavalry advantage. Pratap himself fought in the thick of the engagement and was wounded before being carried from the field, and although the battle ended inconclusively rather than in a clear Mewar victory, it halted Akbar's immediate advance and became the most retold episode of his career.

According to Mewar court tradition recorded in poetry from the seventeenth century onward, Pratap's horse, later named Chetak in these accounts, carried him to safety after he was wounded at Haldighati, leaping a ravine before succumbing to its injuries; contemporary Mughal-era sources do not name or describe the horse in this way, making the story primarily a product of later Rajput literary memory rather than strict documentary record. Following the loss of his ancestral capital Chittorgarh, Pratap based his continued resistance from Aravalli strongholds such as Kumbhalgarh and the valley settlement of Chavand, sustaining a guerrilla campaign over subsequent years that gradually recovered much of Mewar's territory, though Chittor itself was never retaken before his death on 19 January 1597.

His memory is maintained today chiefly through the Maharana Pratap Memorial, also called Pratap Smarak, atop Moti Magri hill overlooking Fateh Sagar Lake in Udaipur, built in 1948 under Maharana Bhagwat Singh and featuring an eleven-foot, seven-tonne bronze statue of Pratap mounted on Chetak, alongside an on-site museum of dioramas and paintings depicting Haldighati and a Japanese rock garden within the same complex. The site, maintained by the Maharana Pratap Smarak Samiti, draws steady visitor traffic as one of Udaipur's established heritage attractions, giving the historical figure a fixed civic presence in the city that succeeded Mewar's old capital.
