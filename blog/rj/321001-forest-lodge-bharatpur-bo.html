Siberian Crane

साइबेरियाई सारस

The Siberian crane (Leucogeranus leucogeranus), known locally in Bharatpur as the "Siberian Koonj" or sometimes confused with the stork-like "Doorga" in older hunting-era accounts, takes its common name from the vast breeding grounds of Arctic and sub-Arctic Siberia, particularly the Yakutia region, from which the central population once journeyed roughly 5,000 kilometres southeast to winter in India. Local naturalists and shikar-era chroniclers in Bharatpur recorded the birds' arrival after the creation of artificial wetlands in the nineteenth century by the Bharatpur royal family, originally developed as a duck-hunting reserve, which inadvertently created the shallow marsh habitat the cranes favoured for feeding on tubers and aquatic invertebrates through the winter months.

Classified today as Critically Endangered by the IUCN, the Siberian crane is among the rarest of the world's fifteen crane species and one of only two crane species that are almost entirely white in adult plumage, the other being the whooping crane. It belongs to a monotypic genus, Leucogeranus, separated from the typical Grus cranes on account of its distinctive long, straight bill adapted for probing wetland mud, its red facial skin extending from the forehead to behind the eye, and its preference for aquatic rather than grassland habitats. The species historically comprised three disjunct populations, eastern, central, and western, of which the central population wintering at Keoladeo was the smallest and the first to disappear, making Bharatpur's record of the bird a documented case study in the collapse of an entire migratory flyway population.

Adults stand around 140 centimetres tall with a wingspan exceeding two metres, and are marked by black primary wing feathers visible only in flight, pale yellow eyes, and reddish legs, distinguishing them in the field from the more numerous sarus and common cranes also recorded at Keoladeo. Regular winter counts at the park, maintained from the 1960s after the sanctuary's formal establishment in 1956 and its designation as a national park in 1981, showed roughly 80 birds arriving in favourable years during that decade, a number that fell sharply over subsequent decades as hunting pressure mounted along the migration corridor through Afghanistan and Pakistan. By the winter of 2000-01 only a handful of birds reached Bharatpur, and in 2001-02 a single pair was the last to be recorded at the park.

A particularly documented incident from the conservation effort involved a young Siberian crane fitted with a satellite transmitter in 1998 by researchers tracking the central population's migratory route; the signal from the juvenile, reared by the last known breeding pair, went silent over central Afghanistan, strongly suggesting it had been shot, while its parents continued on and reached Keoladeo days later without it. This single tracked bird became emblematic of the broader threat facing the central flyway, since widespread legal and illegal hunting across the Russian Federation, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Afghanistan, and Pakistan was ultimately identified as the dominant cause of the population's collapse, more than habitat loss within India itself.

In response, India became a signatory to a Memorandum of Understanding under the Convention on Migratory Species, bringing together range states along with bodies such as the International Crane Foundation and the Wild Birds Society of Japan to pursue captive breeding and satellite-tracking initiatives aimed at reviving the central population, though no cranes have returned to Keoladeo since 2002 despite these efforts. The park's association with the species nonetheless remains formally documented in UNESCO's periodic reporting on the Keoladeo Ghana National Park World Heritage Site, where the Siberian crane's historic presence is cited alongside the site's Ramsar wetland status as part of its original justification for international recognition. Forest department records and interpretation boards at the park continue to reference the bird's decline as a case history for visiting researchers and birdwatchers, even as Keoladeo's current ornithological reputation rests on the roughly 370 other bird species, among them painted storks and spoonbills, that still breed and winter there each year.
