Tea-Elixir of Life

চা - জীবনের অমৃত

The story of Darjeeling tea begins in 1841, when Dr. Archibald Campbell, superintendent of the newly founded hill station and a civil surgeon with the Bengal Medical Service, brought Chinese tea seeds from the Kumaon region and planted them experimentally near his residence at Beechwood. The plants thrived in the cool, well-drained slopes, prompting the colonial administration to expand cultivation through the 1850s. By 1852, the East India Company had established the first commercial gardens at Tukvar, Steinthal and Alubari, and within little more than a decade the district's tea acreage had grown dramatically, laying the foundation for an industry that would come to define the region.

Darjeeling tea occupies a unique place among the world's teas, prized above all for a delicate, fruity muscatel character that tasters and growers alike attribute to the district's combination of high elevation, cool mist, acidic soil and the particular biting action of local leafhoppers on the leaf. Popularly termed the 'Champagne of Teas', it became in 2004 the first Indian product registered under the Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, 1999, following an earlier certification-trademark scheme introduced by the Tea Board in 1999. This legal protection, covering both the word 'Darjeeling' and its official logo, has been central to the industry's long fight against the mislabelling of blended or non-Darjeeling leaf as genuine produce in export markets.

Only tea grown and processed at the 87 gardens registered within the Darjeeling and Kalimpong hills, most of them laid out during the colonial era on terraced slopes between roughly 600 and 2,000 metres, is permitted to carry the Darjeeling name. The bushes, a mix of the original Chinese Camellia sinensis variety and later Assamica hybrids, are harvested by hand across four distinct flushes: a light, floral first flush picked in March and April, a more full-bodied second flush in May and June that yields the signature muscatel notes, a heavier monsoon flush used largely for blending, and a milder autumnal flush closing the season in October and November. This hand-plucking regimen, largely unchanged since the nineteenth century, remains labour-intensive and highly seasonal.

The industry's workforce is overwhelmingly female, with women pluckers making up the bulk of the roughly 55,000 to 60,000 workers employed across the gardens, often continuing a trade passed down through generations of families settled on the estates. This labour-intensive character has made the sector acutely vulnerable to environmental change: a study by the Darjeeling Tea Research and Development Centre at Kurseong found that rising maximum temperatures, declining annual rainfall and falling humidity over recent decades have measurably reduced green-leaf yields, with production dropping by nearly 42 percent between 1993 and 2012 and falling to a record low of around 6.19 million kilograms in 2021, compounding pressures already faced by workers and smallholders alike.

Research into these challenges continues at the Darjeeling Tea Research and Development Centre in Kurseong, which monitors climate trends, soil health and pest activity across the gardens and advises planters on adapting cultivation practices as weather patterns shift. The Tea Board of India, headquartered in Kolkata with a regional office serving the Darjeeling gardens, continues to administer the GI certification and promote the tea in overseas markets even as acreage and output have gradually contracted. Darjeeling's gardens, many still bearing the names given to them by their nineteenth-century founders, remain the only source in the world permitted to sell tea under that name, keeping the hills around the town bound to the crop that first took root there under Campbell's early experiments.
