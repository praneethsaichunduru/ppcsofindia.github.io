Kalighat Temple

কালীঘাট মন্দির

The name Kalighat, from which Kolkata itself is widely believed to derive its own name, refers literally to the ghat or riverbank landing near the temple, historically on the banks of the Adi Ganga, a former channel of the Hooghly River regarded as sacred. The site is counted among the 51 Shakti Peethas of Hindu tradition, the network of shrines said to mark where parts of the goddess Sati's body fell as Shiva carried her corpse across the cosmos after her self-immolation; at Kalighat, tradition holds it was the toes of her right foot. The association gave the locality a sacred identity long before the present temple was constructed, drawing ascetics and pilgrims to its riverside cremation ground and shrine for centuries.

As one of the most important Shakti Peethas in eastern India, Kalighat occupies a central place in Shakta Hinduism, the branch of the faith centred on worship of the Divine Mother in her various forms. Medieval Bengali literature, including references attributed to works from the 15th century onward, mentions a shrine at this location, indicating continuous worship here well before any permanent masonry structure existed. The temple's religious stature placed it alongside other major Shakti Peethas such as those at Kamakhya and Jwalamukhi, and it became, and remains, one of the principal pilgrimage destinations for devotees of Kali in the Bengal region, central to the city's distinct Shakta devotional culture.

The present temple building was constructed in 1809, funded largely through the patronage of the Sabarna Roy Choudhury family, the zamindars historically associated with the villages that grew into Kolkata, replacing a smaller, more modest shrine that had stood on the site earlier. Built in the traditional Bengali Navaratna and ath-chala temple style, the structure features a curved, hut-shaped roofline characteristic of regional temple architecture, with walls patterned in alternating diamond motifs and terracotta panels depicting deities and natural motifs along its cornices. The sanctum houses the temple's central image of Kali, an iconographically unusual murti with three eyes, four arms, and a protruding golden tongue, carved so that the face appears without a conventional lower jaw, a form found at no other Kali shrine in India.

Adjoining the main shrine is the Kundo Pukur, a deep temple tank historically used for ritual bathing, and the Nakuleshwar Shiva temple within the same complex, reflecting the broader pattern seen at many Shakti Peethas where a Bhairava or Shiva shrine accompanies the principal goddess temple. The temple also retains strong associations with animal sacrifice, historically a goat offered before the deity during rituals, a practice that continues at a smaller scale today despite periodic social debate. Kalighat additionally lent its name to the Kalighat painting tradition, a distinctive style of folk watercolour art that developed among artists working near the temple in the 19th century to supply devotional souvenirs to pilgrims, and which later gained recognition as a significant precursor to modern Indian art.

The temple complex underwent its first major modern-era renovation since 1809 under a project launched in 2023 and carried out through 2024, jointly funded by the Kolkata Municipal Corporation, which committed roughly 165 crore rupees, and the Reliance Foundation, which contributed around 35 crore rupees, bringing the total outlay to about 200 crore rupees. The work included desilting and restoring the Kundo Pukur tank, improving drainage and ventilation around the sanctum, separating the surrounding market stalls from the temple precinct with a new boundary wall, and constructing a skywalk linking S.P. Mukherjee Road directly to the temple to ease pilgrim congestion. Administered today by the hereditary Haldar family of temple priests alongside the state and municipal authorities overseeing its upkeep, Kalighat continues to draw dense crowds of worshippers every day of the year, with numbers surging well beyond the ordinary during Kali Puja and Durga Puja.
