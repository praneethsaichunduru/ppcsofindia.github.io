Unicorns of Dooars

ডুয়ার্সের ইউনিকর্ন (গণ্ডার)

The phrase "unicorns of the Dooars" is a poetic epithet rather than a formal zoological name, applied by naturalists and writers to the great one-horned Indian rhinoceros (Rhinoceros unicornis) that inhabits the grassland-forest mosaics of the Dooars, the alluvial plain skirting the eastern Himalayan foothills in northern West Bengal. The name draws on the animal's single prominent horn and thick, plated-looking hide, which together have long invited comparison to the mythical unicorn in European travellers' and colonial-era accounts. Mal HO, the head post office serving the subdivisional town of Malbazar in Jalpaiguri district, lies at the gateway to this landscape, close to Gorumara National Park, and the pictorial cancellation issued from this office commemorates the rhinoceros population sustained in the surrounding reserves.

Rhinoceros unicornis is the largest of the surviving Asian rhino species and is classified as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, its global population concentrated almost entirely in a handful of protected areas across India and Nepal. Within West Bengal, the species holds particular conservation significance because the Dooars reserves, principally Jaldapara National Park and Gorumara National Park, together constitute the second-largest rhino stronghold in India after Kaziranga in Assam. The animal is also a flagship species for the state's wildlife tourism, drawing visitors for elephant-back and jeep safaris, and its presence has shaped forest management priorities across the subdivision in which Mal HO is situated.

Gorumara, originally notified as a reserve forest in 1895 and declared a wildlife sanctuary in 1949 specifically to protect its breeding rhinoceros population, was upgraded to full national park status on 31 January 1992 and now covers roughly 80 square kilometres of riverine grassland and sal forest. Jaldapara, the larger of the two reserves, was similarly built up through decades of habitat protection along the Torsa river and was elevated to national park status in 2014. Both reserves sit within a network of rivers, including the Murti, Jaldhaka, Neora and Mal, that descend from the Himalayas and replenish the tall elephant-grass meadows on which the rhinoceros depends for grazing and cover, while the same terrain supports elephants, gaur, and a wide range of resident and migratory bird species.

The population here suffered a severe historical collapse: nineteenth-century estimates placed at least 240 rhinos across the region, but poaching by organised networks crossing from Assam and the surrounding hill tracts reduced numbers to barely a dozen individuals in parts of the Dooars by the mid-twentieth century, with Jaldapara's count falling to as few as 14 recorded rhinos by 1985. A sustained programme of anti-poaching camps, intensive monitoring and habitat restoration subsequently reversed this decline, and periodic censuses have tracked a steady recovery, with Jaldapara's population rising into the low 300s and Gorumara maintaining a smaller breeding group in the range of 58 to 63 animals in recent counts.

Conservation of the Dooars rhino today is coordinated by the West Bengal Forest Department together with the Jaldapara Wildlife Division and Gorumara Wildlife Division, which conduct joint census exercises, maintain armed anti-poaching camps along the international border tracts, and have introduced measures such as skewed sex-ratio monitoring to address long-term breeding viability. The Mal HO pictorial cancellation, by naming the rhinoceros population the "unicorns of the Dooars," gives philatelic record to a conservation programme that forest officers, researchers and local communities in Jalpaiguri district continue to carry forward through routine patrolling and habitat upkeep in the grasslands around Malbazar.
