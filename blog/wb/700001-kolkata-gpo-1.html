Kolkata City and Famous Places of Kolkata

কলকাতা শহর এবং কলকাতার বিখ্যাত স্থান

Kolkata traces its origins to 1690, when Job Charnock, an agent of the English East India Company, established a trading post on the east bank of the Hooghly River after relocating operations from Sutanuti amid conflict with the Mughal viceroy of Bengal. In 1698 the Company formally acquired three riverside villages, Sutanuti, Kalikata and Gobindapur, from the local landholding Sabarna Roy Choudhury family, and the settlement grew around these hamlets into the city later known as Calcutta. The name itself is generally traced to "Kalikata," possibly derived from the Bengali goddess Kali or from a reference to a canal (khal) and lime-shell wharf (kata) once in the area, though its precise etymology remains debated among historians. A 2003 Calcutta High Court ruling, while not disputing Charnock's role in establishing the trading post, held that settlement at the site predated his arrival, nuancing the city's traditional founding narrative.

As the administrative and commercial capital of British India until the capital was shifted to Delhi in 1911, Kolkata developed into one of colonial Asia's largest and most consequential cities, serving as the seat of the Bengal Presidency and later of undivided Bengal. Its significance extends well beyond administration, since the city anchored the Bengal Renaissance, a sustained 19th and early 20th century movement in literature, social reform, science and the arts that produced figures such as Raja Ram Mohan Roy, Rabindranath Tagore and Swami Vivekananda. Today it functions as the capital of West Bengal and the principal urban centre of eastern India, recognised as the country's hub for Bengali-language literature, art cinema, and classical and folk musical traditions, alongside its continuing identity as a major port and commercial city on the Hooghly.

The city's built environment reflects successive phases of colonial and indigenous architecture, with landmark structures erected over more than two centuries. The Victoria Memorial, among its most recognised monuments, was constructed between 1906 and 1921 to designs by architects William Emerson and Vincent Esch, built of white Makrana marble by the Calcutta firm Martin & Co., and deliberately given a central dome chamber some 64 feet in diameter, intentionally larger than that of the Taj Mahal, at the direction of Viceroy Lord Curzon. Howrah Bridge, a cantilever structure spanning the Hooghly River, was opened in 1943 to replace an earlier pontoon crossing and remains among the busiest cantilever bridges in the world. Kolkata's General Post Office, inaugurated in 1868 near the original site of old Fort William, was designed by Walter B. Granville in a blend of Italian Renaissance and classical styles and stands on ground historically associated with the city's earliest European settlement.

College Street, a roughly one-kilometre stretch lined with secondhand and new bookstalls adjoining the University of Calcutta and Presidency College, has operated as South Asia's largest book market for well over a century and remains closely tied to the city's reputation as a centre of learning and intellectual exchange. The area surrounding it, including the Indian Coffee House, became a historic meeting point for students, writers and political figures through the 20th century. Another distinctive emblem of the city, the yellow Ambassador taxi, based on the Morris Oxford design and manufactured by Hindustan Motors, became the dominant taxicab of Kolkata's streets from the 1950s onward, with its production eventually ceasing in 2014, though many vehicles remain in service as the fleet is gradually phased out under emission and age regulations.

Kolkata GPO itself, located at the city's historic core on B.B.D. Bagh (formerly Dalhousie Square), continues to function as the headquarters of the West Bengal postal circle and remains an active administrative post office handling philatelic and postal services for the city, including the issuance of special commemorative cancellations tied to the region's landmarks and history. The building's heritage status has brought periodic conservation attention from postal and heritage authorities, given its location amid other protected colonial-era structures in the BBD Bagh precinct. As a subject for postal pictorial cancellations, Kolkata draws on this layered identity, pairing its colonial-era monuments with its standing as the cultural capital of Bengal, a combination that keeps the city a recurring theme on commemorative postal material issued from its own GPO.
