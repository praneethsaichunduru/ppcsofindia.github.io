Rajbari Jhargram Palace

রাজবাড়ি ঝাড়গ্রাম প্রাসাদ

The princely house of Jhargram traces its origin to the late sixteenth century, when the Mughal governor Raja Man Singh of Amer, deputed to subdue the forested tract then known as Jungle Mahal, sent his general Sarveshwar Singh to establish control over the area around 1592. Sarveshwar Singh founded a seat of power that took the name Jhargram, meaning a village enclosed by dense forest, and his successors adopted the title "Malla Deb," a name the ruling family has carried ever since. This origin links the Rajbari not to a single founding myth but to a Mughal-era administrative settlement that gradually matured into a hereditary zamindari and, later, a recognised princely estate of eastern India.

As the seat of the Malla Deb Rajas, the Jhargram Rajbari holds significance as one of the principal royal residences of the Jungle Mahal region, an area historically distinguished by its forest-dwelling and tribal communities rather than by the riverine agrarian society typical of much of Bengal. The estate's rulers functioned as zamindars under British paramountcy following the suppression of local resistance, most notably the Chuar rebellion of 1799, in which the Jhargram chief joined neighbouring rulers including those of Bishnupur in opposing East India Company authority before being compelled to submit. The palace that survives today therefore represents less a seat of sovereign kingship than a durable zamindari institution that balanced local tribal governance with colonial administrative obligations.

The present palace building is comparatively recent, construction having begun in 1922 under Raja Narasingha Malla Dev and been completed by 1931. It was designed by Khytish Chandra Biswas, who served as Chief Valuer of the Calcutta Improvement Trust, in an Indo-Saracenic idiom that drew on Italian and Islamic decorative motifs layered over a Bengali zamindari residential plan. The result is a spacious, symmetrically planned structure with ornamented facades, arched verandahs and reception halls suited to both administrative darbars and private royal residence, reflecting the broader fashion among early twentieth-century Bengal estates for combining European classical detailing with indigenous courtyard-based layouts.

A distinguishing feature of the Rajbari is that it remains, in part, the actual residence of the Malla Deb family rather than a wholly musealised relic, with members of the lineage continuing to occupy sections of the complex even as other wings have been repurposed for visitors. This dual identity, part living family home and part public heritage property, sets it apart from several contemporaneous Bengal zamindari houses that were either abandoned or fully converted into institutions after 1947, when the abolition of zamindari rights and princely privileges stripped such estates of their administrative function.

Roughly fifteen rooms on the palace's ground floor have been converted into a heritage hotel operated by the West Bengal Tourism Development Corporation, allowing guests to stay within the original durbar-era architecture while exploring Jhargram's surrounding laterite-soil forests and the town's associated attractions. Maintained jointly through this state tourism body and the resident royal family, the Jhargram Rajbari now functions as a working link between administered heritage tourism and a family history still physically present within its walls.
