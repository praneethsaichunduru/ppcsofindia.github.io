Dooars Tusker

ডুয়ার্সের হাতি

The term 'Dooars tusker' takes its name from the Dooars, a Bengali and Assamese word meaning 'doors,' describing the narrow alluvial plains that form the gateway from the Bengal lowlands into the hill kingdoms of Bhutan and, further east, Sikkim. Alipurduar, the district town after which the post office is named, sits at the heart of this corridor of forest, grassland, and tea estate, and the adult male Asian elephant, or tusker, has long served as the emblematic wild animal of the tract. Early British planters and foresters who cleared the Dooars for tea cultivation from the 1870s onward recorded large resident and migratory elephant herds moving between the Himalayan foothills and the Brahmaputra basin, a pattern of seasonal ranging that gave the region its enduring association with wild elephants.

The Asian elephant (Elephas maximus) occupying the Dooars belongs to one of the largest contiguous elephant populations in eastern India, concentrated across the Eastern Dooars Elephant Reserve, a notified landscape of roughly 1,659 square kilometres encompassing six protected areas: Mahananda, Chapramari, Gorumara, Jaldapara, Buxa, and Jayanti. Jaldapara National Park, covering about 216 square kilometres of grassland and riverine sal forest, and the 760-square-kilometre Buxa Tiger Reserve, which also functions as a recognised cross-border corridor linking Indian and Bhutanese elephant habitat, together anchor the species' presence near Alipurduar. The elephant's status as a Schedule I species under the Wildlife (Protection) Act and a flagship of India's Project Elephant underlines the ecological and conservation weight attached to these populations.

Adult Dooars tuskers typically stand between 2.5 and 3 metres at the shoulder and can weigh over four tonnes, and herds in this landscape are known to undertake long seasonal movements along traditional corridors such as the Jaldapara-Chilapata-Buxa-Nimati route, crossing reserve forest, revenue land, and tea garden sections in search of fodder and water. Surveys covering Darjeeling, Jalpaiguri, and Alipurduar districts have placed the resident elephant population at under 500 animals, a figure representing a small fraction of India's national elephant count yet one responsible for a disproportionately high share of recorded human-elephant conflict incidents nationwide, a consequence of dense human settlement and plantation agriculture pressing directly against forest boundaries.

A particularly severe and well-documented hazard for the Dooars tusker population is the Siliguri-Alipurduar railway line, which cuts directly across several migration corridors and has been the site of repeated fatal collisions between trains and elephant herds, including an incident in which a single goods train strike killed seven elephants, among them calves and females, drawing national attention to the issue. Electrocution from illegally tapped power lines strung around tea estates and injuries from barbed-wire fencing used to keep elephants out of plantations have compounded the toll, with studies estimating roughly fifty human deaths and around ten elephant deaths annually across the wider Dooars landscape from such conflict.

In response, the Union government granted approval after nearly two decades for the formal restoration of historic elephant corridors in the Dooars, including the Jaldapara-Chilapata-Buxa Tiger Reserve-Nimati stretch, while the West Bengal forest department, together with organisations such as the Wildlife Trust of India, has installed early-warning systems, trained rapid-response and anti-depredation squads, and worked with Indian Railways to enforce speed restrictions on elephant-crossing stretches near Alipurduar. The pictorial cancellation issued from Alipurduar SO marking the Dooars tusker reflects this ongoing, closely monitored effort to reconcile the region's tea economy and rail links with the movement of one of eastern India's last substantial wild elephant populations.
