Taj Mahal

ताज महल

The name Taj Mahal is widely understood as a shortened, Persianised form of "Mumtaz Mahal," the title borne by Shah Jahan's favourite consort, Arjumand Banu Begum, meaning roughly "Crown of the Palace." According to court chronicles of the period, including the Badshahnama, the emperor resolved to build an unmatched mausoleum after Mumtaz Mahal died in 1631 in Burhanpur while giving birth to their fourteenth child, her remains later being moved to Agra for permanent interment. The grief-driven commission, begun around 1632, set in motion one of the largest funerary building projects of the Mughal era, with the site chosen on the southern bank of the Yamuna River on land acquired from the Raja of Amber in exchange for other property in Agra.

As a mausoleum complex, the Taj Mahal occupies a distinct place among the world's funerary monuments, standing as the most complete expression of Mughal architecture's synthesis of Persian, Central Asian, and Indian building traditions. It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983, cited for its artistic achievement and its influence on later Indo-Islamic architecture, and it was voted among the New7Wonders of the World in a global poll concluded in 2007. Beyond its architectural standing, the monument functions as the centrepiece of India's tourism economy, drawing several million domestic and foreign visitors annually and generating a substantial share of Agra's local revenue through ticketing, hospitality, and allied trade.

Construction was overseen by a board of architects under the court architect Ustad Ahmad Lahauri, working for Shah Jahan, and involved an estimated twenty thousand workers and craftsmen drawn from across the empire and beyond, including masons, stone-cutters, inlayers, and calligraphers. The main mausoleum, built largely of white marble sourced from Makrana in present-day Rajasthan, was substantially finished by 1643, though ancillary structures and the surrounding complex took roughly another decade to complete. The structure rises to about 73 metres including its central dome and finial, and is flanked by four freestanding minarets, each deliberately tilted slightly outward from the plinth so that, in the event of an earthquake or structural failure, they would fall away from rather than onto the main tomb.

A defining feature of the monument is its decorative inlay work, in which semi-precious and precious stones such as jasper, jade, lapis lazuli, carnelian, and turquoise are set into the marble in floral and calligraphic patterns, a lapidary technique related to pietra dura that required extraordinary precision from specialist craftsmen. The complex is organised around strict bilateral symmetry centred on a reflecting pool within the charbagh-style Mughal garden, with the mausoleum itself deliberately offset to one side of the broader riverfront layout so that the garden's central axis remains uninterrupted. A persistent legend, unsupported by contemporary Mughal records, holds that Shah Jahan intended a mirror-image black marble mausoleum for himself across the river, a story most historians treat as apocryphal rather than documented fact.

Air pollution and industrial emissions in and around Agra have caused the white marble to yellow over time, prompting the Archaeological Survey of India to begin applying a clay-based "mud pack" treatment, using Multani mitti (fuller's earth) mixed with chemicals, since 1994 to lift surface grime without abrasive methods; the dome itself received its first such treatment only in 2021, with officials estimating that fully clearing the discolouration across the monument could take several more years. The ASI continues to administer the site under restrictions aimed at limiting pollution and vibration damage, including a designated Taj Trapezium Zone around Agra that curbs polluting industries. These ongoing, incremental conservation campaigns, rather than any single restoration event, are what keep the monument in the condition in which its millions of annual visitors encounter it today.
