Prayagraj Sangam

प्रयागराज संगम

The name Prayag derives from the Sanskrit prefix 'pra' and 'yaga,' meaning 'place of sacrifice,' a designation recorded since Vedic times for the tract of land where the Ganga and Yamuna meet. Hindu tradition holds that Brahma himself performed the first yajna here after creating the universe, consecrating the confluence as the foremost of pilgrimage sites, or Tirtha Raj. The honorific 'Prayagraj,' meaning 'king among the prayagas,' distinguishes this confluence from four lesser sangams described in Hindu geography, and the city's modern name, restored in 2018 from Allahabad, revived this older religious identity.

As a tirtha, or sacred crossing point, the Sangam occupies a unique place in Hindu cosmology as the meeting of a visible river of 'white' water (the Ganga) and a visible river of darker, greenish water (the Yamuna) with the Saraswati, a river described in the Rigveda that is held to join them invisibly underground. The confluence is counted among the holiest sites in Hinduism and is referenced across the Puranas, the Mahabharata, and the Ramayana, where Rama, Sita, and Lakshmana are said to have crossed it during their exile. Its sanctity underlies its role as the principal venue of the Kumbh Mela, a gathering recognized by UNESCO in 2017 as part of humanity's Intangible Cultural Heritage.

The precise meeting point of the rivers shifts seasonally with water levels and is reached by boat from the ghats, where pilgrims undertake ritual bathing, offer prayers, and perform rites for deceased ancestors. Nearby stands the Akshaya Vat, an ancient banyan tree enclosed within Prayagraj Fort and traditionally worshipped as an imperishable form of Shiva, said to have survived successive floods and destructions of the region. Temporary cities of tents, pontoon bridges, and administrative infrastructure are erected on the exposed riverbed for the Kumbh gatherings, with the 2019 Ardh Kumbh and 2025 Mahakumbh each drawing tens of millions of pilgrims over roughly six-week periods, figures that have made the event one of the largest peaceful human assemblies recorded anywhere.

The 2025 Mahakumbh at the Sangam proved exceptional in scale, with government estimates placing total attendance over the festival's duration at more than 400 million, prompting an unprecedented expansion of ghats, bathing zones, and crowd-management systems along the riverbanks. A crowd crush during the Mauni Amavasya bathing day in January 2025 killed dozens of pilgrims near the Sangam nose, an incident that drew national scrutiny of safety arrangements at the site and led to subsequent reviews of crowd-control protocols for future gatherings. Such episodes underscore the logistical challenge of managing devotional crowds of this magnitude at a geographically constrained riverbank location.

Ahead of recent Kumbh events, the National Mission for Clean Ganga approved dedicated projects under the Namami Gange programme to develop ghats at the Sangam, including Dashashwamedh, Saraswati, and Rasulabad ghats, alongside new sewage treatment infrastructure intended to curb pollution discharge into the confluence. The Prayagraj Mela Authority and Uttar Pradesh state agencies jointly oversee the annual Magh Mela and periodic Kumbh encampments, coordinating with the municipal corporation on sanitation, water quality, and pilgrim amenities. The Sangam today functions simultaneously as a year-round bathing ghat for local devotees and as the staging ground for the state's largest recurring public event, administered through this continuing partnership between religious custom and civic planning.
