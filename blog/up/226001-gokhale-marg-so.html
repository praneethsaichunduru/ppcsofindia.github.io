Rose Flower

गुलाब फूल

The association of Lucknow with the rose traces back to the horticultural passions of the Nawabs of Awadh, who ruled the region from the mid-eighteenth century until annexation by the British in 1856. Persian and Mughal garden traditions, which prized the rose above nearly every other flower for its scent and symbolism, were absorbed and elaborated upon in Awadhi courtly culture, giving the flower a lasting place in the city's self-image as the 'City of Gardens.' Nawab Shuja-ud-Daula's mausoleum complex at Faizabad, known as Gulab Bari or 'Garden of Roses,' survives as a direct architectural testament to this royal fondness for rose cultivation, its grounds historically planted thick with rose bushes said to have calmed the ruler's mind during affairs of state.

Within Lucknow's cultural identity, the rose functions less as a single named landmark than as a recurring horticultural and symbolic motif tied to the city's reputation for tehzeeb, or refined courtly etiquette. The flower's significance rests on its dual role: an ornamental staple of Awadhi garden design, and the raw material for attar and rose water production that fed into Lucknow's cuisine, medicine, and perfumery. Attar, from the Persian 'atr' meaning fragrance, became established in the region as Mughal nobility and, after the Mughal decline, the Nawabs themselves patronised perfumers who distilled rose petals into fragrant oils, a craft that linked Lucknow to the broader Indo-Persian perfume trade centred on nearby Kannauj.

Rose water and rose-derived attar were worked into both the material and sensory culture of the Nawabi court, used to scent clothing, bathing water, and reception halls, and blended into food as a flavouring agent in dishes and sweets still associated with Lucknowi cuisine today, such as gulkand and rose-flavoured mithai. Cultivation of roses for these purposes required extensive garden infrastructure, and Awadhi gardens were typically laid out in the Mughal chaar bagh format, divided into quadrants by walkways and water channels and planted with flowering shrubs alongside fruit trees, with many sited to catch river breezes along the Gomti. This fusion of ornamental and utilitarian horticulture meant that rose cultivation in Lucknow was never purely decorative but was interwoven with courtly economy, craft, and ritual.

A defining modern expression of this legacy is the continued cultivation and public display of roses at institutions such as the CSIR-National Botanical Research Institute (NBRI) in Lucknow, which periodically hosts dedicated rose and gladiolus flower shows at its Botanical Garden, drawing growers and visitors to view cultivated varieties alongside the institute's other floricultural research. The rose has also remained embedded in Lucknow's seasonal horticultural fairs, where local and regional growers exhibit rose varieties as part of wider flower and produce exhibitions that recall the Nawabi era's patronage of ornamental gardening. These events function as a living link between the historic rose gardens of the Nawabs and present-day civic and scientific interest in the flower.

The NBRI itself, established in 1953 as part of the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research network, has broadened its floricultural work well beyond roses, including the development of new lotus cultivars, but its rose displays continue to draw on the same cultural association between Lucknow and fine flower cultivation that the Nawabs established centuries earlier. Local nurseries and the Horticulture Department of Uttar Pradesh likewise maintain rose plantings in public parks and gardens across the city, keeping the flower visible in everyday civic landscaping rather than confined to historical memory. In this way, the rose in Lucknow functions today much as it did under the Nawabs, as both a garden ornament and a marker of the city's claim to horticultural and aesthetic refinement, maintained now through institutional research and municipal gardening rather than courtly patronage alone.
