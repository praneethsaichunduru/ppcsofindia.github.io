First Airmail

पहलौ हवाई डाक

The pictorial cancellation issued at Prayagraj Head Post Office under the theme "First Airmail" commemorates an event that unfolded almost entirely by circumstance rather than design. In early 1911, British aviation promoter Sir Walter Windham organised a flying display as part of the United Provinces Industrial and Agricultural Exhibition at Allahabad, bringing out six Humber-built aircraft to demonstrate powered flight to Indian audiences. Windham then sought, and unexpectedly secured, permission from the postmaster general to carry a batch of mail by air between the exhibition ground and the nearby railway station at Naini, giving the display an improvised postal purpose that would eventually outlast the exhibition itself in historical memory.

The flight that resulted is regarded by philatelists and aviation historians as the first officially sanctioned airmail carriage anywhere in the world, a distinction later endorsed by the Federation Internationale de Philatelie. Its significance lies less in technical achievement than in administrative precedent: a government postal authority, for the first time, authorised and franked mail to be flown rather than carried by road or rail. The cachet applied to the covers read "First Aerial Post, U.P. Exhibition Allahabad 1911," and the mail included items addressed to prominent recipients of the day, among them a letter from Motilal Nehru and correspondence directed to King George V and the Queen of the Netherlands, lending the event an air of genuine official notice beyond its origins as a publicity exercise.

On 18 February 1911, two days ahead of the originally scheduled date, French aviator Henri Pequet, then twenty-three years old, took off from the exhibition's polo ground in a Humber-Sommer biplane powered by a roughly 37-kilowatt (fifty-horsepower) engine. He carried approximately 6,500 letters and postcards packed into the aircraft, covering the nearly ten-kilometre distance across the Yamuna to Naini railway station in about thirteen minutes. Naini was chosen specifically because it lay on the Bombay-Calcutta railway line, allowing the airlifted mail to re-enter the ordinary postal network immediately upon landing, which in practice made the flight a short aerial shortcut within a conventional delivery chain rather than a standalone service.

Among the circumstances that gave the flight its outsized audience was its timing alongside the 1911 Kumbh Mela, which had drawn large crowds to Allahabad and ensured that thousands of spectators, pilgrims and exhibition visitors witnessed the takeoff. The underlying motive was partly charitable, since proceeds from the specially franked mail and souvenir postcards were intended to help fund a local missionary college, a detail that places the flight in a long tradition of aviation stunts doubling as fundraising spectacles. Despite this modest and somewhat improvised beginning, the flight preceded comparable airmail experiments in Britain, France and the United States, giving Allahabad an early and largely unplanned claim to a milestone in global postal and aviation history.

The episode is kept alive today chiefly through philately rather than aviation memorabilia, with surviving covers, postcards and the original exhibition cachet remaining prized items among collectors of Indian aerophilately and featuring in dedicated displays and auction listings worldwide. India Post has marked the centenary and subsequent anniversaries of the flight with commemorative stamps and special covers, and the pictorial cancellation now issued at Prayagraj HO serves as the present-day postal marker of that 1911 event at the very city where it took place. For visitors and collectors alike, the cancellation offers a direct, if modest, link to a flight that lasted only thirteen minutes but gave India an unlikely first chapter in the history of airmail.
