Adiseshan of Lord Ranganatha

ஸ்ரீரங்கநாதரின் ஆதிசேஷன்

The association of Adiseshan with Srirangam is rooted in a legend concerning the Ranganatha idol itself. According to temple tradition, Lord Rama worshipped this image of Vishnu reclining on the serpent and, out of gratitude, gifted it to Vibhishana, brother of Ravana, after the war in Lanka, on condition that it never touch the ground. While resting beside the Kaveri river during his journey south, Vibhishana set the idol down for a ritual, after which it could no longer be lifted; Vishnu declared his wish to remain there permanently as Ranganatha, facing south as a boon to his devotee. The place that grew around this spot came to be called Srirangam, from the Tamil arangam, meaning an isle.

Within this narrative, Adiseshan, the many-hooded serpent also known as Ananta Shesha, is not a peripheral figure but the very seat and vehicle of the central deity, giving the temple's iconography its distinctive identity. The image of Vishnu reclining full length upon the serpent's coils, a posture Sanskrit texts term anantashayana, represents the god at rest upon the cosmic waters between cycles of creation and dissolution. Srirangam's Ranganathaswamy Temple is reckoned the foremost of the 108 Divya Desams sacred to Vaishnavism and is widely regarded as the largest functioning Hindu temple complex in the world, a status that rests in large part on the scale of the shrine built to house this one reclining image.

The temple as it stands today, spread across some 156 acres within seven concentric walled prakarams and ringed by 21 gopurams, took shape over many centuries, with Sangam-era Tamil literature already attesting to worship at the site and major building phases carried out under the Chola, Pandya, Hoysala, and Vijayanagara dynasties between roughly the twelfth and sixteenth centuries. The Vijayanagara period, spanning 1336 to 1565, contributed some of the temple's most celebrated construction, including the hall popularly called the Thousand-Pillared Mandapa, which in fact contains 953 granite pillars, each carved with mythical creatures, warriors, and courtly figures. Vijayanagara rulers also restored the deity to the sanctum in 1371 after it had been moved for safekeeping during earlier invasions.

A particularly striking feature tied to the temple's Vijayanagara building campaign is its southern Rajagopuram, begun under Achyuta Deva Raya but left unfinished after his death, remaining incomplete for more than four hundred years. Its completion was finally undertaken in the twentieth century by Sri Vedanta Desika Yatheendra Mahadesikan, the forty-fourth jeeyar of the Ahobila Matha, with construction spanning roughly eight years before the tower, rising about 239.5 feet, was consecrated on 25 March 1987. At 13 storeys it is counted among the tallest temple gopurams in Asia, a landmark visible for miles across the Kaveri delta and a fitting gateway to the sanctum where Adiseshan bears the reclining Ranganatha at the complex's innermost core.

The temple continues to function as a living religious institution administered under the Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments framework of the Tamil Nadu government, with daily rituals, periodic renovation and consecration works, and a calendar of festivals that includes the elaborate Vaikunta Ekadasi celebrations drawing large numbers of pilgrims each year. Scholarly and conservation interest in the site remains active as well, with institutions such as the French Institute of Pondicherry having documented its inscriptions and architectural history in detail. The serpent couch of Adiseshan, rendered in stone at the heart of this vast temple city, thus remains the fixed point around which Srirangam's religious life, its annual festival calendar, and its ongoing conservation work continue to be organised.
