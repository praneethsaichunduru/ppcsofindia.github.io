Deities of Deivanai, Murugan & Valli

தெய்வானை, முருகன் மற்றும் வள்ளி ஆகியோரின் திருவுருவங்கள்

Tiruttani's association with Murugan's two consorts stems directly from the deity's own mythology. After vanquishing the demon Surapadman, Murugan is said to have married Deivanai, daughter of Indra, at Thiruparankundram, while his second marriage, to Valli, the earth-born foster daughter of the tribal chieftain Nambirajan, took place here at Tiruttani when the god appeared before her in the guise of a hunter. The hill shrine thus commemorates a distinct episode in the deity's romantic and martial cycle, separate from the war narrative itself, and the Tamil name Tiruttani is understood locally as a mark of this sanctified ground where the courtship and union of Valli unfolded.

As the fifth of the six Arupadaiveedu, the sacred abodes of Murugan spread across Tamil Nadu, the Tiruttani temple holds a settled place in Saiva Siddhanta devotional geography alongside Palani, Swamimalai, Thiruparankundram, Pazhamudircholai and Thiruchendur. Its particular distinction lies in presenting Murugan flanked by both Deivanai and Valli in the same sanctum, a configuration read by devotees as the reconciliation of the god's celestial and earthly unions into a single completed household. The temple is further venerated as one of the sites referenced in early Tamil devotional poetry, lending it standing within the Sangam-era literary tradition in addition to its ritual importance.

The temple stands atop Thanigai hill and is reached by a flight of stone steps popularly counted at 365, said to correspond to the days of the year, with the complex itself built in Dravidian style across four precincts beneath a five-tiered gopuram. Epigraphic evidence places the earliest surviving stone inscriptions at the site to the ninth-century Pallava period, including records associated with the ruler Aparajita Varman, while the tenth-century Chola king Parantaka I is credited with endowments that supported the development of the main sanctum. Subsequent expansion under the Vijayanagara rulers between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries added larger mandapas and extended the temple's outer precincts, giving the present structure its layered, multi-period character.

The temple's literary pedigree is anchored by its mention in the Tirumurukāṟṟuppaṭai, a devotional Sangam-period poem attributed to Nakkeerar that catalogues the six abodes of Murugan, making Tiruttani one of the few Murugan shrines with a textual record extending back to the early centuries of the Tamil literary corpus. In more recent times the temple has drawn notice for the scale of its festival offerings, with reports of eleven-day festival collections exceeding one crore rupees in cash alongside several hundred grams of gold donated by devotees, reflecting the shrine's continuing pull as a major pilgrimage and offering centre in northern Tamil Nadu rather than a purely historical monument.

The temple today functions under the administration of the Tamil Nadu Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Department, which oversees its daily rituals, the annual Brahmotsavam, the Valli Kalyanam re-enactment of the Tiruttani marriage, and the Aadi Krithikai and Kanda Sashti observances that draw the largest crowds of pilgrims each year. Postal recognition of the site through a pictorial cancellation tied to the Tiruttani Sub Office places the twin-consort iconography of Deivanai, Murugan and Valli within the department's broader record of temple-linked cancellations issued across Tamil Nadu, giving collectors a documented philatelic marker for a shrine whose active festival calendar keeps it functioning as a living place of worship rather than a static heritage site.
