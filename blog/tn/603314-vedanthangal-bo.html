Painted Stork

ஓவியப் புலியன்

The painted stork (Mycteria leucocephala) takes its common name directly from its appearance, its white body plumage overlaid with a distinctive black pectoral band and tertial feathers tinged a soft rose-pink, a feature unique among the storks of its genus. Adults stand roughly 93 to 102 centimetres tall, weigh between two and five kilograms, and are marked by a bare orange-yellow face and a long, heavy, slightly down-curved yellow bill used for tactile foraging in shallow water. Juveniles lack the adult's pectoral band and carry duller brown plumage until maturity. The species ranges across the wetlands of the Indian subcontinent and parts of Southeast Asia, favouring shallow freshwater marshes, irrigation tanks, and flooded agricultural land where it can wade and sweep its bill through the water in search of prey.

Within India, the painted stork holds particular significance as one of the principal breeding species associated with Vedanthangal, a seasonal wetland near Chengalpattu in Tamil Nadu that is widely regarded as the country's oldest bird sanctuary. Protection of the site dates to 1798, when the Collector of the then Chingleput district issued an order prohibiting the shooting of birds after local villagers petitioned against indiscriminate hunting by British soldiers stationed nearby; formal recognition and government funding for the sanctuary followed in the twentieth century. This long, community-initiated history of protection, predating most organised wildlife conservation efforts in India, gives the painted stork's presence at Vedanthangal an institutional as well as an ecological dimension, tying the species directly to one of the subcontinent's earliest recorded examples of locally driven conservation.

Painted storks are colonial tree-nesters, typically establishing mixed heronries in a small cluster of five or six trees standing in or near open water, with a single breeding colony sometimes holding seventy to a hundred nests built from sticks and twigs. In southern India, including at Vedanthangal, breeding begins later than in the north, generally commencing in October or November and continuing through March, with December and January marking the peak of nesting activity. Clutches usually contain one to five eggs, incubated for about a month, with chicks fledging after a further two months in the nest. At Vedanthangal the storks nest alongside spot-billed pelicans, openbill storks, ibises, egrets, and herons on the flooded babul and barringtonia trees within the tank, which together host tens of thousands of waterbirds during the peak season.

A defining feature of the Vedanthangal colony is its reliance on a shallow irrigation tank rather than a natural lake, the water level of which is carefully managed to keep the nesting trees partially submerged and inaccessible to ground predators such as jackals, a practical arrangement that has made the site unusually productive for breeding waterbirds over more than two centuries. The painted stork remains classified as a species of Least Concern by the IUCN, though its numbers are closely tied to the availability and quality of such wetland habitats, many of which have been lost elsewhere in India to drainage and development, making well-managed sites like Vedanthangal disproportionately important to the species' regional population even though it is not currently under direct threat of extinction.

The sanctuary today falls under the Tamil Nadu Forest Department, which regulates water levels in the tank, monitors nesting activity, and maintains a watchtower and interpretation centre used by researchers and visitors to observe the colony without disturbing it. Annual waterbird censuses conducted at Vedanthangal have recorded painted stork numbers running into several thousand individuals in a good breeding season, making the species one of the most reliable indicators of the sanctuary's ecological health from year to year. The postal pictorial cancellation issued from Vedanthangal BO draws on this long association between the painted stork and the tank it breeds in, marking a wetland that still functions as a working, state-managed sanctuary rather than a preserved historical curiosity.
