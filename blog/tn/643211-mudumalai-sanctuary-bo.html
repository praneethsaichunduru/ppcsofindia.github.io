Indian Bison (Gaur)

இந்திய காட்டெருமை (காளை)

The gaur, popularly known in India as the Indian bison, takes its common name from a conflation with the American and European bison despite belonging to the genus Bos, the same genus as domestic cattle, making the label a historical misnomer rather than a taxonomic one. Its species name, Bos gaurus, derives from the Sanskrit-rooted vernacular "gaur" used across much of its South Asian range. The animal is distributed across the forested hill tracts of peninsular and northeastern India and parts of Southeast Asia, with one of its most significant strongholds lying in the contiguous forests of the Western Ghats, including the Mudumalai Wildlife Sanctuary in the Nilgiris district of Tamil Nadu, after which the nearby post office and this pictorial cancellation take their theme.

As the largest living species of wild cattle, surpassing even the American bison and wild water buffalo in bulk, the gaur occupies a position of considerable ecological and cultural significance in India. It is listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, a classification in place since 1986 reflecting long-term population decline linked to habitat loss and historical hunting, and it receives the highest legal protection available domestically under Schedule I of the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972. India holds the great majority of the global gaur population, with the Western Ghats landscape spanning the Wayanad, Nagarhole, Mudumalai and Bandipur forests recognised as one of the species' most important surviving refuges, giving the sanctuary a prominent place in national conservation planning.

Adult gaur are massively built animals, with males standing roughly 1.65 to 2.2 metres at the shoulder and reaching body weights of up to 1,500 kilograms, while females are somewhat smaller. The species is distinguished by a muscular dorsal ridge running from the shoulders partway down the back, a dewlap at the throat, sturdy white-stockinged lower legs, and a high convex boss on the forehead between a pair of outward- and upward-curving horns. Predominantly browsers and grazers of grasses, bamboo shoots, and forest herbage, gaur favour the mixed deciduous and moist forest habitats found across Mudumalai, where the sanctuary's mosaic of grassland and woodland provides the grazing and cover the species requires through the dry and wet seasons alike.

A notable episode in the conservation history of the species was the first systematic population estimation exercise for the Indian gaur, carried out in 2020 by the Tamil Nadu Forest Department specifically across the Nilgiris forest division, which placed the local population at around 2,000 animals, underscoring both the scale of the Mudumalai-Nilgiris population and the importance of dedicated monitoring methods distinct from the camera-trap based tiger census used elsewhere in the reserve. The exercise marked a shift toward treating the gaur as a conservation priority species in its own right rather than only as incidental tiger prey, and it has since informed how forest managers track herd numbers and habitat use within the sanctuary.

Mudumalai itself, established in 1940 as South India's first wildlife sanctuary and later folded into Project Tiger in 2007 as a core critical tiger habitat, remains under the administration of the Tamil Nadu Forest Department, which also oversees the Theppakadu elephant camp and the network of anti-poaching camps that help sustain the forest corridor linking the sanctuary to Bandipur, Wayanad and Nagarhole. This corridor connectivity is what allows gaur herds, along with elephants and tigers, to move seasonally across state boundaries in search of forage and water. The sanctuary's gaur population, sustained by this wider landscape-level protection effort, continues to be one of the more reliable wildlife sightings offered to visitors travelling the Ooty-Mysore road through the reserve.
