Manapad Lighthouse

மணப்பாடு கலங்கரை விளக்கம்

Manapad takes its name, by local tradition, from a Tamil rendering connected to the sandy coastal terrain of this fishing village on the Gulf of Mannar, a stretch of shoreline that drew Portuguese sailors into its waters from the early sixteenth century onward. The Manapad Point Lighthouse that now stands here is a comparatively recent addition to a settlement whose maritime identity long predates it, built to mark a hillock promontory that had already served, for centuries, as a landmark for ships navigating the shallow reefs and currents separating the Indian mainland from Sri Lanka across the gulf.

The lighthouse belongs to India's network of coastal navigational aids administered by the Directorate General of Lighthouses and Lightships, and its significance rests chiefly on its role in safeguarding fishing craft and merchant shipping along a historically hazardous and commercially active coastline. Manapad itself holds a distinct place in the ecclesiastical history of South India, sometimes described as a 'Little Jerusalem' of the Tamil coast on account of the string of Portuguese-era Christian sites clustered around the village, chief among them the Church of the Holy Cross, which anchors the settlement's identity alongside its maritime economy.

The lighthouse was established by the British colonial administration in 1888, after the area had already gained importance as a salt export centre that drew British personnel to build quarters there before the tower itself went up. It is a masonry structure roughly 18 metres in height, painted in red and white diagonal bands and set upon a hillock rather than directly at sea level, which is unusual among lighthouses on this coast and gives it an extended range of visibility over the surrounding reef-strewn waters. Its light source has changed over time, beginning as a gas-fed lamp from 1888 until around 1900, later converted to photovoltaic operation that remained in use until 1994, after which it was modified again to run on an electric lamp.

A short distance from the lighthouse stands the Church of the Holy Cross, whose origins are tied to a popular local legend: in 1540, a Portuguese ship's captain, saved from a storm while rounding the Cape of Good Hope, is said to have fashioned a cross from his vessel's broken mast and planted it on the shoreline hillock in gratitude, a relic venerated locally as the 'Captain's Cross.' Jesuit missionary Francis Xavier is recorded to have stayed in Manapad between 1542 and 1544, reportedly using a cliffside cave overlooking the sea as a retreat during his missionary journeys through South India, and the grotto associated with this tradition was enlarged into a proper church building in 1581.

In 1583, a relic believed to be a fragment of the True Cross, sent by Pope Gregory XIII, reached the church at Manapad, and it continues to be brought out for public veneration each year during a festival held from the first to the fourteenth of September, drawing large numbers of pilgrims to the village. The lighthouse, meanwhile, remains under the operational charge of the Directorate General of Lighthouses and Lightships as a functioning aid to navigation rather than a monument alone, and it is this pairing of an active coastal safety installation with a centuries-old pilgrimage church that gives Manapad its somewhat unusual character among Tamil Nadu's southern coastal villages.
