Great Nandi

பெரிய நந்தி

The Great Nandi at Gangaikondacholapuram derives its significance from the story embedded in the name of the town itself. After Rajendra Chola I led a victorious military expedition to the banks of the Ganges in the early eleventh century, he commemorated the feat by founding a new capital and calling it Gangaikonda Cholapuram, literally "the city of the Chola who conquered the Ganga." The temple built to mark this achievement, dedicated to Shiva as Brihadisvara, placed a colossal image of Nandi, the bull that serves as Shiva's vahana and gatekeeper, directly before the sanctum, so that the first and most lasting impression of the emperor's new capital for any visitor approaching the shrine was this seated guardian facing the deity.

Within Hindu temple iconography, Nandi is far more than decorative livestock: he is Shiva's devoted mount and the eternal witness stationed opposite the sanctum, embodying unwavering devotion and dharma. The Gangaikondacholapuram Nandi belongs to one of the most historically important temple complexes in South India, since the shrine it guards is itself part of the Great Living Chola Temples, a group of three eleventh- and twelfth-century temples inscribed together on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2004 alongside the Brihadeeswarar Temple at Thanjavur and the Airavatesvara Temple at Darasuram. The temple and its Nandi are maintained today as a centrally protected monument under the Archaeological Survey of India.

The temple was raised around 1035 CE under Rajendra Chola I, son and successor of Raja Raja Chola I, who had earlier built the great Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur. Rather than simply replicating his father's monument, Rajendra's architects produced a structure on a somewhat smaller footprint but with a vimana whose curvilinear profile and sculptural finish many art historians rank above the Thanjavur tower. The Nandi positioned before the sanctum is a large stone monolith, commonly cited at roughly fifteen feet in length, eleven feet in height and about eight feet in breadth, carved facing the towering sanctum that houses one of the largest Shivalingas in South India, reportedly standing close to four metres tall.

A distinguishing feature of the Gangaikondacholapuram complex is how much of its surrounding imperial city has vanished while the temple and its Nandi endure almost in isolation. Once the thriving capital of the Chola empire for more than two centuries, with a palace complex, reservoirs and a planned urban layout, the city around the temple was gradually abandoned and plundered for building material in later centuries, most notably when the Nayaks of Thanjavur reportedly dismantled parts of the old capital to build the Kallanai-area irrigation works and other structures. Excavations at the nearby site of Maligaimedu have since uncovered brick remains identified with the royal palace, offering physical confirmation of the city that once surrounded the Nandi and its temple.

Conservation of the site today falls to the Archaeological Survey of India, which manages the temple as part of the joint UNESCO inscription covering Thanjavur, Gangaikondacholapuram and Darasuram, while the State Department of Archaeology has carried out separate excavation seasons at Maligaimedu to recover further traces of the vanished Chola capital. The temple continues to function as an active place of worship even as it draws historians and visitors studying Chola statecraft and architecture, with the Great Nandi still seated before the sanctum as the one element of Rajendra Chola's capital that has never needed to be excavated to be seen.
