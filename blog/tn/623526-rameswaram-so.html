Sri Ramanathaswamy Temple Corridor

ஸ்ரீ இராமநாதசுவாமி கோயில் நடைபாதை

Rameswaram derives its sanctity from the Ramayana tradition, which holds that Rama built a causeway (Rama Setu) to Lanka from this point and, on his return after defeating Ravana, worshipped Shiva here to atone for the sin of killing a Brahmin, since Ravana was himself a learned Brahmin sage. The name "Ramanathaswamy" literally means "Lord of Rama," referring to the Shiva lingam Rama is said to have installed and worshipped at this spot, making the shrine one of the few in India directly linked in legend to an incarnation of Vishnu honouring Shiva.

The temple ranks among the twelve Jyotirlinga shrines of Shiva and is simultaneously one of the four Char Dham pilgrimage sites of Hinduism, a dual distinction shared by no other Shiva temple in the country. This pairing draws pilgrims following both the Shaiva and Vaishnava traditions, and the site's ritual geography, encompassing the temple, the adjoining Dhanushkodi shore, and the sea itself, gives it a standing comparable to Kashi Vishwanath and Badrinath in the national pilgrimage circuit administered under Tamil Nadu's Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments framework.

The temple's present form, including its towering gopurams and vast prakaras, took shape over several centuries, with the Pandya and later Vijayanagara-era rulers contributing early structures before the Setupati rulers of Ramnad undertook the major expansions. The celebrated third corridor, known as the Chokkattan Mandapam, was largely built in the eighteenth century, with substantial work credited to Muthuramalinga Setupati, who ruled from 1763 to 1795; the outer corridor alone measures roughly 400 feet along its eastern and western arms and about 640 feet along the north and south, rising close to 7 metres in height and supported by an estimated 1,212 carved granite pillars.

A ritual distinct to Rameswaram is the sequence of bathing in all 22 theerthams, or sacred wells, within the temple precincts before proceeding to the sanctum, a practice pilgrims traditionally begin only after an initial dip at Agnitheertham, the seashore just outside the eastern gopuram where Rama is believed to have bathed to cleanse himself after the war. Temple priests draw water from each well in sequence and pour it over pilgrims, with each theertham reputed to have a distinct taste and mineral quality, a feature that has drawn scientific curiosity alongside religious devotion.

The temple continues to be administered by the Tamil Nadu Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Department, which oversees its upkeep and the daily rituals conducted by resident Brahmin priests, several of whose families have served the shrine for generations. In recent years the corridor and gopurams have featured prominently in national coverage of Rameswaram, including during high-profile state visits that showcased the site's architecture to international delegations. The corridor remains open to the steady flow of pilgrims who walk its length between rituals, making it as much a functioning passage of daily worship as a monument of historical record.
