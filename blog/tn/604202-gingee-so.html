Gingee Fort

செஞ்சி கோட்டை

The origins of Gingee Fort are tied to local legend and early medieval chieftains. Tradition credits a shepherd named Ananda Kon with discovering treasure around 1200 CE on the western hill and using it to raise a small fortification called Anandagiri, giving the Kon dynasty its foothold in the region from roughly 1190 CE. The site subsequently came under the Cholas and then the Hoysalas in the fourteenth century, before being absorbed into the expanding Vijayanagara Empire in 1383 CE. The name Gingee is itself believed to derive from these early rulers, and the hill that bears Ananda Kon's name, also called Rajagiri or Kamalagiri, remains the tallest and most prominent of the fort's three summits.

As a fortified complex, Gingee belongs to the category of great peninsular Indian hill forts, and it earned lasting renown among European travelers and chroniclers as the 'Troy of the East' for its seemingly unconquerable defences. By the early fifteenth century it had grown into one of the most important cities of the Vijayanagara Empire, ranked alongside Hampi and Madurai as a major administrative and military centre. Its significance has been reaffirmed in the present day: in July 2025, Gingee Fort was inscribed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site, included as the sole Tamil Nadu representative within the twelve-fort serial nomination titled the Maratha Military Landscapes of India.

The fort's most extensive construction took place under the Vijayanagara Nayakas of Gingee, who strengthened the existing defences and added palaces, temples, granaries, and stables through the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. The complex spans three hills, Rajagiri, Krishnagiri, and Chandrayandurg, each separately fortified and linked by massive granite ramparts reported to run nearly sixty feet wide, encircled in places by a moat some eighty feet across. Rajagiri alone rises close to 800 metres and houses a citadel, granary, and gymnasium accessible by a steep rock-cut stairway, while the surrounding plain holds gateways, a Kalyana Mahal, and a cavalry stable that together illustrate the scale of the garrisoned township the Nayakas maintained within the walls.

The fort's reputation for impregnability was tested repeatedly, most famously when the Maratha ruler Shivaji captured it in July 1677 during his southern campaign, reportedly calling it the most impregnable fortress in India and ordering its mud ramparts rebuilt in granite. Decades later it became the last redoubt of Maratha resistance under Rajaram I, who fled there after the Mughal execution of Sambhaji, holding out against a Mughal siege commanded by Zulfiqar Khan that dragged on for roughly eight years, from 1690 to 1698, among the longest sieges in Mughal military history before the fort finally fell.

Following its capture by the Mughals, Gingee passed successively to the Nawabs of Arcot, the French, and finally the British, each of whom used its strategic position during the eighteenth-century Carnatic Wars before the fort lost its military role under colonial rule. Today the site is maintained as a protected monument by the Archaeological Survey of India, which carries out periodic conservation surveys and repair work across its gateways, temples, and fortifications, and its 2025 UNESCO inscription has brought renewed attention to its upkeep as a heritage destination. Gingee Fort now functions primarily as a major tourist and heritage site in Villupuram district, drawing visitors to climb Rajagiri's ramparts and explore a garrison town that once withstood some of the longest sieges in Indian history.
