Punnai Leaves and Peacock

புன்னை இலைகள் மற்றும் மயில்

The name Mylapore itself is said to derive from "Mayilai," from the Tamil words myil (peacock) and oor (town), recalling a legend in which Goddess Parvati took the form of a peacock to worship Shiva beneath a punnai tree at this very site. In the same tradition, Shiva is venerated locally as Punnaivana Nathar, "lord of the punnai forest," a title reflecting the dense stands of Calophyllum inophyllum that are said to have once covered the area before the city grew around them. The pairing of punnai leaves and peacock on this cancellation is thus a direct visual shorthand for the founding legend of Mylapore, binding the locality's very name to its most sacred tree and its most sacred bird in a single image.

Botanically, the punnai (Calophyllum inophyllum), also called the Alexandrian laurel or, in Sanskrit, nagchampa, is a hardy coastal evergreen bearing glossy leathery leaves and clusters of fragrant white, four-petalled flowers with yellow stamens. In Tamil Sangam literature the tree is placed squarely within the neythal, the seashore landscape-category of classical poetry, indicating that communities along Tamil Nadu's coast lived amid punnai groves as far back as two millennia ago. Beyond its ornamental and devotional value, the tree held real economic importance: its timber was prized by Chola shipwrights for naval construction, its seeds yield a medicinal oil still used in traditional remedies, and its flower is counted among the ashtapushpam, the eight flowers traditionally offered to Shiva in daily worship.

Within the Kapaleeshwarar Temple complex, a specific punnai tree standing in the northern prakaram (corridor) is revered as the sthala vriksham, or sacred tree, of the shrine, and is popularly described as the oldest living tree in Chennai. Temple tradition holds that it was beneath this very tree that the goddess Karpagambal performed her worship of Shiva in peacock form, making the tree not merely incidental greenery but the physical site of the locality's founding myth. The temple around it, dedicated to Shiva as Kapaleeshwarar and to his consort Karpagambal, is understood to have been built by the Pallava dynasty around the 7th century CE, with the site itself referenced in Tamil devotional texts dating to the 6th century CE, placing the sacred grove and its associated worship well before the structures seen today.

The temple's history includes a significant disruption: the original shrine, believed to have stood closer to the Bay of Bengal, was destroyed by Portuguese colonists in the mid-16th century during the construction of São Tomé, and the present structure in its current location was substantially rebuilt under the Vijayanagara rulers later that century. Kapaleeshwarar Temple is counted among the Paadal Petra Sthalams, the 275 Shiva temples glorified in the verses of the Nayanmar saints, underscoring Mylapore's standing as one of Tamil Shaivism's most storied pilgrimage centres even after its physical relocation and rebuilding. The peacock motif recurs throughout the temple's iconography and festivals, visually reinforcing the Parvati-as-peacock legend that gives the neighbourhood its name alongside the punnai tree in its courtyard.

Today the punnai population along Tamil Nadu's coasts and riverbanks has markedly declined over recent decades owing to demand for its timber, making the surviving temple specimen in Mylapore an object of particular botanical and conservation interest, occasionally cited in local ecological writing and heritage-tree documentation efforts around Chennai. The Kapaleeshwarar Temple itself remains an active place of daily worship administered under the Tamil Nadu Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Department, drawing pilgrims to its car festival and other annual observances centred on the tank and the tree-shaded prakarams. The postal pictorial cancellation issued from Mylapore Head Post Office thus pairs a living botanical relic with a mythic bird, giving philatelists a compact emblem of the neighbourhood's founding story each time it is struck on an envelope.
