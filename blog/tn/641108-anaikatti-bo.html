Salim Ali Centre for Ornithology and Natural History (SACON)

சலீம் அலி பறவையியல் மற்றும் இயற்கை வரலாற்று மையம் (சேகான்)

The idea of a dedicated national institute for ornithology took shape in the 1980s as a personal mission of Dr. Salim Ali, the self-taught naturalist who came to be known across India as the 'Birdman of India' for his decades of fieldwork documenting the subcontinent's avifauna. Ali hoped to see a centre of excellence built around the discipline he had spent his life advancing, but the proposal remained unrealised at the time of his death in 1987. The Ministry of Environment and Forests, working together with the Bombay Natural History Society (BNHS), the institution Ali had long been associated with, carried the plan forward, and in 1990 the Government of India formally established the centre, naming it the Salim Ali Centre for Ornithology and Natural History in his memory.

Coimbatore was chosen as the institute's headquarters because of its proximity to the biodiversity-rich Western Ghats, a region of global conservation significance, and SACON began functioning in 1991 out of a rented building at Kalampalayam on Siruvani Road before its present campus was developed. As an autonomous institution under India's Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, SACON occupies a distinct niche among the country's scientific bodies: it is the principal centre devoted specifically to avian science, combining taxonomy, ecology, and conservation biology with a mandate to train researchers and advise on environmental policy, including environmental impact assessment work commissioned by government and industry.

The present campus, located about 24 kilometres north-west of Coimbatore city adjacent to the Coimbatore South Reserved Forest at an elevation of roughly 600 to 610 metres above sea level, was assembled through two land purchases from private owners, 34.04 acres in 1994 and a further 20.09 acres in 1995, bringing the institute's grounds to approximately 54.73 acres. The centre is organised into research divisions covering avian ecology and endangered species conservation, wetlands, environmental contamination, biodiversity modelling, and nature education, supported by laboratories, a herbarium, and a reference library built up over more than three decades of fieldwork across Indian habitats.

A defining feature of SACON is that its own campus has become a documented object of study: surveys have recorded 177 species of birds within its grounds, including roughly 100 resident species alongside winter and local migrants, together with 402 species of flowering plants from 84 families, among them Red Listed sandalwood trees, and 107 species of butterflies, representing close to a third of all butterfly species known from the Western Ghats. This concentration of life at the foothills site has made the campus itself a working field laboratory for the institute's ecologists, in addition to its formal research stations elsewhere in the country.

SACON's scientists continue to contribute to national assessments of threatened bird populations, wetland health, and the ecological effects of development projects, publishing findings through its annual reports and journals and training postgraduate and doctoral researchers who go on to staff India's wildlife and forestry institutions. The centre also maintains close institutional ties with the Bombay Natural History Society, which shared in its founding, and with the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve authorities given its location on the reserve's edge. Functioning today much as Ali had envisioned, as a dedicated home for ornithological science, SACON keeps its research grounded in the same Western Ghats landscape that first justified Coimbatore's selection as its site.
