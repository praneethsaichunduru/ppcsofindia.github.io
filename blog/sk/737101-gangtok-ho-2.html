Khangchendzonga National Park

खाङ्चनजङ्घा राष्ट्रिय निकुञ्ज

Mount Khangchendzonga carries two parallel origin narratives among the communities who live in its shadow: the Lepcha know it and its surrounding valleys as Mayel Lyang, a hidden paradise, while Tibetan Buddhist tradition, drawing on fourteenth-century Terma 'revealed treasure' texts, describes the region as Beyul Demazong, a concealed land of sacred and secret treasures. Both traditions hold that the mountain is the seat of Dzonga, the guardian deity of Sikkim, believed to protect the land and its people, and both continue to shape how the area is approached and ritually marked by the Lepcha and Bhutia communities settled around it.

Named for Kangchenjunga, the world's third-highest peak at 8,586 metres, Khangchendzonga National Park was formally established on 27 August 1977 with an initial area of 846 square kilometres, later enlarged to roughly 1,784 square kilometres by the late 1990s to bring in the summit massif, its glacier system and adjoining lowland forest. In July 2016 it became India's first UNESCO Mixed World Heritage Site, honoured simultaneously under natural criteria for its biodiversity and landscape and under cultural criteria for its association with the Lepcha and Bhutia sacred-landscape traditions, placing it among a small global list of properties recognised on both counts.

The park falls under the jurisdiction of Sikkim's Forest, Environment and Wildlife Management Department and is protected under the Wildlife Protection Act, 1972, which affords it the highest statutory conservation status in India. Spanning Mangan and Gyalshing districts, it rises from around 1,220 metres to the 8,586-metre summit, one of the steepest altitudinal gradients of any protected area in the world, and shelters roughly eighteen glaciers, among them the Zemu Glacier, one of the largest in Asia, alongside some seventeen high-altitude lakes. This range of elevation supports habitats from subtropical broadleaf forest through temperate and alpine zones to permanent snow, sustaining species such as the snow leopard, red panda, musk deer and numerous Himalayan pheasants and rhododendrons.

The mountain has never been climbed to its literal highest point out of deference to local belief that the summit is sacred ground; the first successful 1955 British expedition led by Joe Brown and George Band stopped a few feet short of the true top, a gesture subsequent expeditions have generally honoured. The Rathong Chu river valley within the park is likewise treated as sacred by Lepcha residents and in Tibetan Buddhist practice, a status that in the 1990s helped block a proposed hydroelectric project in the valley following sustained local opposition, reinforcing the link between religious reverence and the forest's continued ecological intactness.

Around 35,000 people, chiefly from Lepcha, Bhutia, Nepali and Limboo communities, live in some 44 villages in the park's surrounding landscape, and rituals such as the Lepcha Nay-Sol and the Bhutia Pang Lhabsol continue to be performed in honour of the mountain deity. The IUCN World Heritage Outlook's 2020 assessment rated the site's conservation outlook as 'good', the only Indian natural or mixed World Heritage property to receive that rating at the time. Managed jointly through state forest administration and community stewardship arrangements with neighbouring villages, Khangchendzonga National Park today functions as both a strictly protected wilderness and a living pilgrimage landscape, with local institutions and the forest department coordinating to keep trekking, grazing and ritual access compatible with its conservation mandate.
