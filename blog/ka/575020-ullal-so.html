Rani Abbakka Devi

ರಾಣಿ ಅಬ್ಬಕ್ಕ ದೇವಿ

Rani Abbakka Devi, also remembered as Abbakka Chowta, belonged to the Chowta dynasty, an indigenous Tuluva ruling house of coastal Karnataka that followed the matrilineal Aliyasantana system of inheritance practised among the Bunt community. Under this custom, succession passed through the female line, and it was her uncle, Tirumala Raya, who crowned her queen of the port town of Ullal around 1525, a subsidiary capital of the Chowtas whose principal seat lay at Puttige. Trained from a young age in swordsmanship, archery, horsemanship and statecraft, she assumed charge of a small but strategically positioned coastal territory at a time when Portuguese naval power was tightening its grip on Malabar and Kanara trade routes.

As queen, Abbakka Devi holds a distinctive place in Indian history as one of the earliest documented women to lead sustained armed resistance against European colonial power, predating the more widely known resistance movements of later centuries by nearly three hundred years. Though she and the Chowta royal family were adherents of Jainism, she extended patronage to the Hindu Someshwara temple at Ullal, whose deity, Somanatheshwara, the dynasty had adopted as a family protector, and she is additionally credited with supporting local arts and literature during her reign. Her refusal to pay tribute to the Portuguese, who by the mid-sixteenth century controlled much of the pepper and spice trade along the western coast, set her apart from neighbouring chieftains who had largely submitted to Portuguese commercial demands.

Hostilities began in earnest after Abbakka stopped the customary tribute payments, prompting a Portuguese expedition in 1555 and a further engagement in 1556 led by Admiral Dom Álvaro da Silveira, which ended inconclusively without securing Portuguese control of Ullal. To offset the technological advantage of Portuguese cannon and naval vessels, she cultivated alliances with regional powers, most notably the Zamorin of Calicut, and maintained ties with local Muslim merchant communities whose trading interests were likewise threatened by Portuguese monopolies. These alliances gave her access to manpower and naval support that allowed a comparatively small coastal kingdom to withstand repeated incursions from one of the most formidable maritime forces operating in the Indian Ocean at the time.

The most celebrated episode of her reign came in 1568, when a Portuguese force under General João Peixoto, dispatched on the orders of Viceroy António de Noronha, succeeded in briefly occupying Ullal and entering the royal court. Abbakka escaped capture and took shelter overnight in a mosque, then rallied roughly two hundred of her soldiers and launched a counterattack before dawn, in which Peixoto was killed and some seventy Portuguese soldiers were taken captive. The episode became the defining proof of her military resolve, reinforcing her reputation as 'Abhaya Rani,' and later accounts describe her continuing to take the field personally against subsequent Portuguese attempts well into her later years.

Rani Abbakka's memory is actively sustained today through the annual Veera Rani Abbakka Utsava, held in Ullal and in Bengaluru, which commemorates her resistance with cultural programs, awards and processions organised with state government participation, alongside statues erected in her honour and an Indian Coast Guard patrol vessel, ICGS Rani Abbakka, named for her in recognition of her naval-era defiance. Historians and local researchers continue to piece together her story largely from Portuguese colonial records and Tulu oral folk traditions such as the Paddanas, since contemporaneous indigenous documentation is limited, making her one of the rare instances where a coastal kingdom's resistance survives chiefly through the written accounts of its adversaries.
