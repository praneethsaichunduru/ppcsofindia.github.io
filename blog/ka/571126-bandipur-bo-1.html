The tiger's face and tiger's paw motifs used on postal cancellations originating from Chamarajanagara district draw directly on the area's identity as one of India's foremost tiger landscapes, centred on the Bandipur and Biligiri Rangaswamy Temple (BRT) Tiger Reserves. Bandipur traces its protected status back to 1931, when the Maharaja of Mysore set aside roughly 90 square kilometres of forest as the Venugopala Wildlife Park, a private hunting and game reserve maintained by the Mysore royal family. The name Bandipur itself is drawn from the forest tract bordering the former princely state, long valued for its dense teak and dry deciduous cover sheltering tiger, elephant, and gaur populations.

In 1973, the Venugopala Wildlife Park was substantially enlarged, with roughly 800 additional square kilometres brought under protection, and the combined area was declared Bandipur Tiger Reserve, one of the original nine reserves notified when the Government of India launched Project Tiger that year under Chief Minister D. Devaraj Urs. At the time of notification the reserve held an estimated 12 to 13 tigers. The reserve today forms a core part of the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve, designated in 1986, which links Bandipur with Mudumalai in Tamil Nadu and Wayanad in Kerala to form one of the largest contiguous blocks of tiger habitat in Asia, underscoring the ecological rather than merely ornamental basis for the tiger's appearance on local postal iconography.

The BRT Tiger Reserve, lying in the same district and taking its name from the Biligiri Ranganathaswamy Temple on the hilltop within it, was separately declared a tiger reserve in 2011. It covers approximately 574.82 square kilometres, divided into a core zone of about 359.10 square kilometres and a buffer of 215.72 square kilometres, and functions as an ecological link between the Western and Eastern Ghats, a transition reflected in its unusual mix of both moist and dry-forest flora and fauna within a single reserve. Together with Bandipur, it anchors Chamarajanagara's claim to be among the most significant tiger landscapes in peninsular India.

A distinguishing feature of BRT is the long-standing presence of the Soliga, an indigenous tribal community inhabiting the Biligiri Rangana Hills and the adjoining Male Mahadeshwara Hills, who depend on the forest for honey, gooseberry, lichen, tubers, and other non-timber produce gathered under traditional practice. In a 2011 ruling, the Soliga became the first forest-dwelling community in India to have its community forest rights formally recognised within a declared tiger reserve, a judgment frequently cited in Indian wildlife law as reconciling strict core-area protection with the residency rights of a forest-dependent population, and one that set a precedent later invoked in other reserves.

Tiger numbers across the two reserves have grown markedly since Project Tiger's early decades: forest department assessments in recent years have placed Bandipur's resident population in the range of 126 to upwards of 160 tigers, with estimates of tiger usage of the wider landscape running higher still, alongside a recovering elephant population now numbering over 1,500 within the reserve. Both reserves are managed under Karnataka's Forest Department in coordination with the National Tiger Conservation Authority, with camera-trap monitoring and periodic all-India tiger estimation exercises now forming the backbone of their management, work that keeps Chamarajanagara's tiger and paw-print cancellations tied to an active, ongoing conservation programme rather than a static emblem of the past.
