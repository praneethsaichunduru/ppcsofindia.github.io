Shri 1008 Parshwanath Bhagawan

ಶ್ರೀ ೧೦೦೮ ಪಾರ್ಶ್ವನಾಥ ಭಗವಾನ್

The Parshvanath temple at Kamthana, a village roughly ten kilometres from Bidar town, is dedicated to Parshvanatha, the twenty-third Tirthankara of Jainism, and is venerated locally under the honorific title Shri 1008 Parshwanath Bhagawan. The numeral '1008' is a conventional Jain epithet of reverence rather than a historical count, used across temple dedications in India to signify completeness and the totality of the Tirthankara's spiritual attributes. Kamthana's shrine is counted among the lesser-known Jain tirthas of the Deccan, its sanctum built into a rock-cut cave setting that distinguishes it from the free-standing temple architecture more common to the region, and local tradition holds that the site functioned for generations as a place of quiet pilgrimage long before it drew wider attention.

Within Jain tradition, Kamthana carries particular weight as a site associated with sallekhana, the vow of fasting unto death undertaken by ascetics and devout householders seeking a disciplined end to life, which made the location a preferred destination for Jain munis and shravakas in earlier centuries. The temple's core icon, a seated Parshvanatha image rendered in paryankasana (the cross-legged meditative posture) and sheltered beneath a canopy of a seven-hooded serpent representing Dharanendra, is reported by regional Jain heritage sources to stand about 115 centimetres tall and 55 centimetres wide, and to have been installed approximately nine centuries ago. Scholars examining the sculpture have noted the presence of a diamond-shaped Srivatsa mark on the chest of the image, an iconographic feature more typical of North Indian Jain sculpture than of the South, suggesting lines of artistic or devotional influence reaching Bidar from outside the immediate Deccan tradition.

Bidar's prominence as the later capital of the Bahmani Sultanate from 1432, and afterward the seat of the Barid Shahi dynasty, shaped a regional landscape dominated by fort architecture, mosques and royal tombs, yet Jain institutions such as the Kamthana shrine persisted alongside this Islamicate political centre. Local accounts describe the temple as having once formed part of a functioning gurukul, a traditional residential school that for an extended period offered religious and secular instruction to students without charge, reflecting the broader pattern by which Karnataka's Jain communities paired temple patronage with educational activity. Accounts associated with the site further note that the temple and its gurukul continued to receive protection and upkeep under the Nizams of Hyderabad, whose territory encompassed Bidar for much of the eighteenth through twentieth centuries, allowing the shrine to survive despite shifts in regional political control.

A distinctive feature recorded at the cave sanctum is a continuously burning lamp whose flame is reflected across roughly a thousand small mirrors fixed to the surrounding rock walls, an arrangement intended to multiply the lamp's light within the enclosed cave space and to heighten the sense of sanctity inside the shrine. The temple's profile within the wider Jain community was substantially revived after the Digambara monk Acharya Shri Shrut Sagar Maharaj rediscovered and drew renewed attention to the site in 1987, prompting restoration interest and a resumption of organised worship that had lapsed over the preceding decades. Since that rediscovery, Kamthana has again become a point of pilgrimage rather than a largely forgotten rural cave shrine.

The temple today observes an annual three-day rathayatra, or chariot festival, typically held in February, which draws Jain pilgrims from across Karnataka and neighbouring states and remains the principal public occasion marking the site's religious calendar. Coverage of the event by Jain community publications describes organised processions, ritual worship of the Parshvanatha image, and gatherings of monks and lay devotees converging on the otherwise quiet village for the duration of the festival. Maintained in the present day largely through the efforts of local Jain trusts and the pilgrims who sustain its annual festival, the Kamthana shrine functions less as a monumental architectural landmark than as an active, modestly scaled pilgrimage cave whose religious calendar continues to bring Bidar district's Jain heritage into yearly focus.
