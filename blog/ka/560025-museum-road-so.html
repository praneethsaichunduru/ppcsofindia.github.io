Sandesha Museum

The building on Museum Road that now houses Sandesha, the Museum of Communication, takes its name from an earlier occupant: the thoroughfare itself was christened Museum Road because the structure served, from around 1865, as Bengaluru's first Government Museum, founded by the Scottish surgeon and naturalist Edward Balfour. Long before it became a postal landmark, the colonial-era building passed through several institutional lives, reportedly serving at different times as a jail superintendent's residence and premises connected to a bank, before the Postal Department took it over for use as a divisional post office, a function it continued to serve for more than a century.

Sandesha belongs to the small category of dedicated communication-heritage museums in India, run under the aegis of the Department of Posts rather than as a conventional city museum, which gives it particular significance for philatelists and postal historians. Its core identity rests on tracing the full arc of Indian communication, from indigenous courier and signalling systems used before colonial rule through the establishment of organised postal administration under British India and the Kingdom of Mysore, up to modern telecommunication technology, making it a reference point for understanding how the subcontinent's vast distances were bridged by messaging infrastructure over roughly two centuries.

The museum occupies a structure estimated to be about 150 years old, built in the characteristic colonial civic style of nineteenth-century Bengaluru, and was adapted for its present purpose with public access opening in 2019 after a period of restoration and curatorial planning by postal authorities. Inside, the museum is organised into six themed rooms, each carrying its own name and focus; the stamp gallery is arranged around three sub-themes, covering the 1857-to-1947 freedom-struggle period described as the 'Birth of the Nation', a section on Indian culture depicted through philately, and a dedicated display on 'Mysore Anche', the indigenous Mysore postal system that operated between 1721 and 1889 before its absorption into the imperial postal network.

A distinguishing feature of Sandesha is its Samparka gallery, which assembles tangible artefacts of communication technology rather than paper exhibits alone, including a hand-cradle telephone, an adding-and-listing machine, a Morse transmitter, a mail-bomb detector, a Petromax lantern once used for night deliveries, vintage Army postal uniforms, and a quadrant scale historically used for weighing mail. This juxtaposition of philatelic material with mechanical and administrative objects sets the museum apart from a standard stamp gallery, letting visitors trace how sorting, weighing, transmission, and security practices evolved alongside the stamps and covers more typically associated with postal heritage.

The museum functions alongside a working post office on the same premises, allowing visitors to buy commemorative stamps, covers, and postcards and post them on site, a feature that keeps the location active as a philatelic touchpoint rather than a purely static display. Entry is free, and the museum is open daily except Sundays and public holidays, drawing school groups and heritage enthusiasts as part of Bengaluru's broader postal heritage trail alongside sites such as the General Post Office. Administered by the Department of Posts as part of its public outreach efforts, Sandesha now serves as the city's primary venue for introducing younger visitors to the history of India's postal and telecommunications network.
