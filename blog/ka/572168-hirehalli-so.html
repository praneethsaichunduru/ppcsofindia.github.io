Pinchi Basadi, Mandaragiri

ಪಿಂಚಿ ಬಸದಿ, ಮಂದರಗಿರಿ

The name Pinchi Basadi derives directly from the structure's distinctive form: 'pinchi' refers to the fan of peacock feathers carried by Digambara Jain monks as an implement for gently whisking away insects without harming them, and the temple at the foot of Mandaragiri hill near Tumakuru was deliberately built in the shape of this feather-fan, giving it its popular name (it is also called the Guru Mandir or, informally, the peacock temple). The structure stands as a memorial to Acharya Shantisagar (1872-1955), a revered Digambara ascetic credited with reviving rigorous monastic practice within the tradition in the twentieth century, and its unusual silhouette was conceived specifically to evoke the pinchi he and fellow munis carried.

Mandaragiri itself, meaning 'Mount Mandara' after the cosmic mountain of Hindu and Jain cosmology, is among Karnataka's lesser-known but historically significant Digambara Jain hill-shrine complexes, standing roughly fifteen kilometres from Tumakuru town just beyond Hirehalli along the Tumakuru-Bengaluru highway. The hilltop carries a cluster of basadis generally dated to the Ganga and later Hoysala periods, making the site part of the same devotional and architectural lineage as Shravanabelagola and other hill-based Jain tirthas of the region, while the Pinchi Basadi/Guru Mandir at its base represents a modern addition to that older sacred landscape rather than an ancient shrine itself.

An inscription dated to 1160 CE, recorded at the site (Epigraphia Carnatica, Tumkur taluk), documents that a basadi and an accompanying tank known as Padmavati Kere were constructed through the patronage of Machiakka, wife of Ereyanga, who served as chief general under the Hoysala king Narasimha I; a second tank, Maidala Kere, also lies on the hilltop. The hill today holds several surviving Digambara shrines dedicated to tirthankaras including Parshvanatha, Suparshvanatha and Chandraprabha, built across different periods and enclosed within a walled precinct, reflecting the layered history of patronage the hill received over centuries. The Guru Mandir, by contrast, was erected far more recently, taking shape over about eighteen months on a base measuring roughly 60 by 40 feet.

Completed and formally inaugurated over a three-day ceremony held from 9 to 11 May 2014, the Guru Mandir rises to a height of approximately 81 feet, its tapering, curved profile designed to mimic the sweep of a peacock feather fan, and organisers at the time described it as the first structure of its kind in Jain architectural history, a claim publicised as a 'Unique World Record' during the inauguration. Inside, the sanctum houses a white marble image of Acharya Shantisagar, about five feet in height, depicted seated in padmasana, making the building simultaneously a devotional shrine and a commemorative monument to a single modern monastic figure rather than to a tirthankara.

The Mandaragiri complex as a whole continues to draw pilgrims from the Digambara Jain community of the Tumakuru region, with an annual pooja mahotsava held at the site each January drawing organised gatherings of devotees to both the hilltop basadis and the newer Guru Mandir below. Maintenance of the shrines and the surrounding grounds has largely been sustained through local Jain trusts and community contributions rather than a single formal archaeological authority, keeping the site active as a living place of worship even as its older inscriptions remain of continuing interest to epigraphists studying Hoysala-era Jain patronage in the district.
