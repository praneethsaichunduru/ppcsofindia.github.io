Inscription Stones of Bengaluru

ಬೆಂಗಳೂರಿನ ಶಾಸನ ಶಿಲೆಗಳು

The inscription stones scattered across Bengaluru and its surrounding villages are not a single monument but a dispersed body of stone records, written chiefly in Old Kannada script, that chieftains and local rulers set up over roughly a millennium to mark grants, deaths in battle, and religious endowments. The most celebrated among them, discovered in the precincts of the Nageshvara Temple at Begur and dated to around 890 CE, records a conflict remembered as the 'battle of Bengulooru' and is widely cited as the earliest surviving written occurrence of the city's name, predating by more than six centuries the 1537 founding traditionally credited to Kempe Gowda I. The stone was identified and published in the early twentieth century by the epigraphist R. Narasimhachar in the Epigraphia Carnatica series, where it appears as Bangalore inscription number 174.

Collectively these stones belong to the broader Kannada epigraphic tradition, one of the most extensive in India owing to the durability of granite as a recording medium and the administrative habit, shared by successive dynasties, of inscribing land grants and memorials directly onto rock rather than perishable materials. Their significance is chiefly historiographical: they supply the only contemporaneous documentation for a period of Bengaluru's past that predates reliable chronicles, allowing historians to reconstruct a sequence of political control running from the Western Gangas through the Cholas and the Hoysalas before the region passed into the Vijayanagara orbit. Several are protected as inscribed monuments and hero stones, and clusters of them, such as those at Begur and Yelahanka, are recognised as locally important heritage sites even though they lack the singular visibility of a temple or fort.

The stones take a small number of recurring forms, the most common being the viragallu or hero stone, a carved slab commemorating a warrior who died in combat, typically depicting the fight, the hero's ascent to a celestial reward, and a final panel showing him in the presence of a deity. The Begur hero stone itself commemorates two sons of the local Ganga-era chieftain Nagattara, Pervvonashetti and Buttanashetti, who were killed in the fighting it describes, and the surrounding temple complex holds one of the densest concentrations of such records in the region, with inscriptions ranging from the ninth-century Ganga period through Chola and Hoysala additions up to Vijayanagara-era grants of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Other early examples, such as a hero stone found near present-day K. R. Puram linked to the Ganga king Sripurusha, who reigned roughly between 726 and 788 CE, push the documented presence of organised settlement in the area back even further.

A particularly instructive case is the Hebbal-Kittayya inscription, which names a Ganga-period chieftain and was for years at risk of being lost amid ordinary urban redevelopment before local residents and heritage volunteers intervened to protect it, illustrating the precarious condition of many such stones, which frequently sit unprotected on roadsides, in private compounds, or inside working temples rather than in fenced archaeological enclosures. Awareness of this vulnerability has grown alongside Bengaluru's rapid expansion, since construction activity, widening roads, and the neglect of small shrine precincts have historically destroyed or displaced stones that once stood where they were originally erected, so that many known inscriptions today survive only because they were photographed, measured, or formally recorded before being moved.

Renewed documentation efforts led by heritage researchers and citizen groups over the past two decades, working alongside the Karnataka Itihasa Academy and local conservation volunteers, have catalogued dozens of these stones across the city's taluks and pressed for the protection of sites like Begur and Hebbal, in some cases raising funds to build small enclosures or markers around individual stones that previously stood exposed. The Nageshvara Temple complex at Begur, where the 890 CE inscription survives, remains under the protection of the Archaeological Survey of India and continues to draw researchers and heritage walkers rather than general tourist traffic, a quieter form of attention suited to records whose value lies less in scale than in the precise historical dates and names they preserve.
