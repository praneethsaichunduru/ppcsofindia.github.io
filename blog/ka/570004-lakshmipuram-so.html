Silver Jubilee Clock Tower

ಬೆಳ್ಳಿ ಜುಬಿಲಿ ಗಡಿಯಾರ ಗೋಪುರ

The Silver Jubilee Clock Tower stands at a prominent junction facing the north gate of Mysore Palace, and its name records precisely the occasion it was built to mark: the completion of twenty-five years, or the silver jubilee, of the reign of Nalwadi Krishnaraja Wadiyar IV over the princely state of Mysore. It was formally inaugurated on 8 August 1927, with the funds for its construction reportedly raised through contributions from the palace staff of the period, making it as much a tribute from the maharaja's own establishment as an official civic commission.

As a commemorative clock tower erected to honour a ruling monarch's jubilee, the structure belongs to a well-established genre of public monuments built across colonial-era and princely India in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, often funded by subscription and placed at prominent civic junctions. In Mysuru it holds particular local significance as one of two historic clock towers in the city, familiarly distinguished by size: the Silver Jubilee Clock Tower is known locally as Dodda Gadiyara, or the 'big clock tower,' as opposed to the smaller Dufferin Clock Tower near K.R. Circle, underscoring its standing as the more prominent of the city's timepieces.

Rising to a height of about seventy-five feet (roughly 24 metres), the tower was built using sandstone brought from Rajasthan and is generally described as combining Indo-Saracenic elements with touches of Dutch Gothic styling, consistent with the eclectic architectural vocabulary favoured in Mysore's Wadiyar-era public buildings. Its clock faces are notable for displaying numerals in the Kannada script rather than the standard Roman numerals seen on most Indian clock towers of the period, a detail that ties the monument directly to the regional identity of the kingdom it commemorates. The clock mechanism itself, along with its bell measuring some five feet in height, was imported from France, reflecting the preference of the Mysore court for European craftsmanship in prestige public works.

The tower has also drawn attention in more recent decades for structural concerns rather than purely ceremonial ones: cracks noticed in parts of the masonry prompted municipal and heritage experts to inspect the monument and assess its condition, an episode that highlighted the ongoing challenge of maintaining a nearly century-old brick-and-stone structure standing amid heavy traffic at a busy road junction. Such inspections have generally resulted in repair and reinforcement work aimed at preserving the tower's structural integrity without altering its historic appearance.

The clock tower today functions as both a working timepiece and a recognised heritage landmark within Mysuru's urban core, falling under the general upkeep of local civic and heritage authorities who have periodically carried out repairs following reported damage to its structure. It is commonly referenced alongside the Dufferin Clock Tower as one of the city's two surviving colonial-era clock towers, giving residents and visitors a readily identifiable point of comparison when discussing Mysuru's public clocks. Positioned within sight of the Mysore Palace's northern approach, it remains a functioning fixture of the street it occupies, still used by passers-by to check the time much as it was when first wound up in 1927.
