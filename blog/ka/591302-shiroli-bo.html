Bhimgad Wildlife Sanctuary

ಭೀಮಗಡ ವನ್ಯಜೀವಿ ಅಭಯಾರಣ್ಯ

Bhimgad Wildlife Sanctuary takes its name from Bhimgad Fort, a hill fort built in the seventeenth century under Shivaji that once guarded the mountain passes linking the Deccan plateau to the Konkan coast and Goa. The surrounding forest tract, long known locally through this fort, was formally notified as a wildlife sanctuary by the Karnataka government in December 2011, after years of advocacy by conservationists who had identified the area as holding a uniquely vulnerable bat population. The sanctuary spans roughly 19,042 hectares of forest in Khanapur taluk of Belagavi district, close to the Jamboti village area and hugging the Karnataka-Goa border within the Western Ghats.

The sanctuary's core significance rests on the Barapede cave system, which was, at the time of the species' rediscovery, the only known breeding site anywhere in the world for the Wroughton's free-tailed bat (Otomops wroughtoni). First described in 1913 and feared extinct for decades thereafter, the bat was rediscovered in these caves in the early 2000s, with the known colony estimated at under fifty individuals, among the rarest mammal populations documented anywhere. This single fact effectively drove the sanctuary's creation: conservation groups argued successfully that protecting the cave and its surrounding catchment was essential to prevent the extinction of a species found nowhere else, giving Bhimgad a conservation rationale built around a single, highly localised colony rather than a wider assemblage of large mammals.

Beyond the bat colony, Bhimgad forms part of the contiguous forest belt connecting Karnataka's Western Ghats landscape to Goa, functioning as a recognised tiger corridor between the two states even though it does not sustain a resident tiger population of its own. The sanctuary's moist broadleaf forest also marks the source of the Mahadayi (Mhadei) river, fed by a cluster of roughly thirty springs rising within its boundaries before the river flows onward into Goa as the Mandovi, a hydrological role that has made Bhimgad central to the long-running Mahadayi water-sharing dispute between the two states. The area's bird diversity is substantial enough that BirdLife International has listed the Bhimgad forests as an Important Bird Area, alongside documented populations of endemic amphibians and reptiles characteristic of the Western Ghats biodiversity hotspot.

A particular point of note is that, unlike many of Karnataka's other forested protected areas, Bhimgad is not known to support a resident population of Asian elephants, with some wildlife surveys explicitly recording its large-mammal assemblage as largely complete except for that species. This distinguishes the sanctuary's conservation profile from more elephant-centred reserves elsewhere in the state and reinforces why its protected status has rested so heavily on the cave-roosting bat population and its forest catchment rather than on large terrestrial mammals, an unusual basis for a wildlife sanctuary's designation in the Indian protected-area system.

Management of the sanctuary falls under the Karnataka Forest Department, which oversees the Barapede cave area along with the surrounding reserve forest blocks that were consolidated into the protected area at notification. The sanctuary continues to feature in ongoing inter-state proceedings over the Mahadayi river's waters, keeping its springs and catchment forests under sustained official and judicial attention even as field conservationists monitor the Wroughton's free-tailed bat colony as an indicator of the cave ecosystem's health.
