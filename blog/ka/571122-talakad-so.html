Panchalinga Darshan

ಪಂಚಲಿಂಗ ದರ್ಶನ

The term Panchalinga Darshana literally denotes the "viewing of five lingas," a rite tied to the ancient Kaveri-side town of Talakad in present-day Mysuru district. According to the prevailing local belief, the five shrines of Vaidyanatheshwara, Pathaleshwara, Maruleshwara, Arkeshwara and Mallikarjuna (at nearby Mudukuthore) represent five faces or aspects of Shiva, together forming what devotees call the Pancha Pathi. Tradition holds that visiting all five lingas in a single day, with the ritual worship at each temple commencing simultaneously, bestows exceptional merit, a belief that has turned an otherwise sand-covered pilgrimage town into the centre of one of Karnataka's most anticipated periodic religious observances.

Within Shaiva practice, Panchalinga Darshana belongs to the broader South Indian custom of linking clusters of Shiva shrines along a river into a single sacred circuit, comparable to other pancha-kshetra traditions found elsewhere in the subcontinent. Its distinguishing feature is rarity: the full darshana is traditionally timed to a Kartika Krishnapaksha Amavasya (the new moon of the Kartika month) that falls on a Monday, an alignment that panchangam calculations show recurring only irregularly, sometimes after a decade and sometimes after a considerably longer gap, rather than on a fixed annual or even strictly twelve-year schedule as is sometimes popularly stated. This astronomical rarity, rather than any single founding date, is what has made the event a major draw for pilgrims from across Karnataka and neighbouring states whenever it falls due.

Talakad itself is an exceptionally old settlement on a sharp bend of the Kaveri, once home to more than thirty temples built up under successive ruling houses, including the Gangas, Cholas, Hoysalas and later the Vijayanagara and Mysuru Wadiyar establishments, several of whose inscriptions and structural additions survive in the surrounding temple complexes. Of the five Panchalinga shrines, the Vaidyanatheshwara temple is the largest and most architecturally intact, standing largely clear of sand, while the Pathaleshwara and Maruleshwara temples lie within the dune-covered core of the old town and the Arkeshwara and Mallikarjuna temples stand somewhat apart on its outskirts, a layout that shapes the specific route pilgrims walk and travel during the darshana.

A defining and unusual circumstance of the site is that much of old Talakad, including parts of these temple precincts, lies buried under wind-driven sand dunes that, by some historical accounts, have advanced at a rate of several feet a year, particularly during the southwest monsoon. Local memory attributes this burial, along with centuries of dynastic misfortune for the Mysuru Wadiyar rulers, to a curse pronounced in the early seventeenth century by Rani Alamelamma, who is said to have drowned herself in the Kaveri near the village of Malangi after a dispute with Raja Wodeyar over temple jewellery, invoking the three-part curse that Talakad turn to sand, Malangi become a whirlpool, and the Mysuru royal line fail to produce direct heirs, a legend still cited locally alongside the Panchalinga tradition itself.

In recent decades the Karnataka state government, through the Department of Archaeology, Museums and Heritage along with the Muzrai (religious endowments) administration, has periodically carried out sand-clearance and temple conservation work at Talakad, including efforts that partially uncovered temple structures ahead of major gatherings, such as the Panchalinga Darshana held in December 2020. Pilgrim numbers at these occasions have historically run into the hundreds of thousands over the several days of associated festivities, placing considerable logistical demands on local administration for crowd management, sanitation and transport between the five shrine sites. Today the Vaidyanatheshwara temple functions as the site's principal year-round place of worship and the practical starting point for visitors, with the other four shrines drawing sustained pilgrim traffic chiefly in the lead-up to and during the rare years when the full five-temple darshana is formally observed.
