Black Buck Sanctuary

ಕೃಷ್ಣಮೃಗ ಅಭಯಾರಣ್ಯ

The grasslands that now form the Ranebennur Blackbuck Sanctuary in Haveri district carried no special protective status for much of the twentieth century; until the 1960s the tract was heavily overgrazed and denuded by cattle grazing and firewood collection, leaving only scattered pockets of blackbuck surviving amid agricultural fields. Recognising the rapid decline of this once-common antelope across the Deccan plains, the Karnataka Forest Department began a plantation and habitat-restoration programme in the area during the early 1970s, and on 17 June 1974 the tract was formally notified as a wildlife sanctuary dedicated chiefly to the protection of the blackbuck, giving the site both its name and its founding purpose.

The blackbuck (Antilope cervicapra) is the sole extant species of its genus and among the fastest land animals in India, with mature males developing the ringed, spiralling horns and dark dorsal coat that distinguish them from the fawn-coloured females and juveniles. Ranebennur's population is significant not merely as a tourist attraction but as one of the more successful instances of species-specific conservation in Karnataka, the sanctuary having been carved out expressly to arrest a decline driven by hunting, poaching and the steady conversion of open scrubland to cropland across the species' historic range in peninsular India. The animal's cultural resonance, as sacred in parts of Hindu tradition and bound up with the Bishnoi community's long resistance to its killing, lends the sanctuary a significance that extends beyond pure wildlife management into questions of shared heritage.

The sanctuary covers roughly 119 square kilometres, split into two physically separate eastern and western blocks rather than a single contiguous reserve, and is further divided administratively into a core zone of about 14.87 square kilometres and a larger buffer-cum-tourism zone of around 104 square kilometres. Vegetation across the reserve is dominated by dry scrub and thorn forest interspersed with Eucalyptus plantations raised during the Forest Department's early restoration drive, a mixed landscape that nonetheless provides the open sightlines and grass cover blackbuck herds require for grazing and predator evasion. Sustained protection allowed numbers to recover substantially from the depleted base of the 1970s, with census counts in the mid-2000s recording the population at more than 6,000 animals, among the higher concentrations recorded at any single blackbuck sanctuary in the country.

Beyond the blackbuck itself, Ranebennur has carried particular significance for ornithologists as one of the last known Karnataka habitats of the critically endangered great Indian bustard, a heavy ground-dwelling bird that once shared these grasslands with the antelope; sightings of the bustard here, however, have not been confirmed since around the early 2000s, and conservationists now regard it as likely to have disappeared from the sanctuary, a loss that is often cited as a cautionary marker of how fast India's grassland specialists can vanish even inside a protected area. The sanctuary additionally supports wolves, another species dependent on the same open, undisturbed grassland that benefits the blackbuck, making the reserve a rare multi-species refuge for fauna adapted to a habitat type that receives far less conservation attention than forest.

Today the sanctuary is managed by the Karnataka Forest Department as part of the state's wildlife protected-area network, with the buffer zone opened to regulated eco-tourism including jeep safaris and a watchtower used for viewing herds at close range, while the core zone remains more strictly reserved for the animals themselves. Its proximity to Ranebennur town, itself linked to the postal circuit through the Ranebennur SO post office whose pictorial cancellation commemorates the sanctuary, has made the reserve a frequently visited stop for travellers moving between Hubballi and the districts further south. The sanctuary's continued upkeep by state forest officials, balancing visitor access against the needs of a still-recovering grassland ecosystem, keeps it functioning as a working conservation site rather than a static monument to a vanished landscape.
