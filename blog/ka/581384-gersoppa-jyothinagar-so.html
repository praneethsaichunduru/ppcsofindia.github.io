Shri Chaturmukha Digambara Basadi

ಶ್ರೀ ಚತುರ್ಮುಖ ದಿಗಂಬರ ಬಸದಿ

The name Chaturmukha Basadi derives directly from its architectural plan: "chaturmukha" means "four-faced" in Sanskrit, describing a shrine with four identical entrances oriented to the cardinal directions, each leading inward to a shared sanctum. The temple stands at Gerusoppa (also spelled Gersoppa), near the banks of the Sharavati river in Honnavar taluk of Uttara Kannada district, a site that developed under its Jain rulers into a locally renowned pilgrimage town, often remembered through the legacy of the queen credited with commissioning the structure, Rani Chennabhairadevi.

The basadi is a Digambara Jain temple of considerable historical standing, built as the principal religious monument of Gerusoppa during the period when the town served as a Jain political capital under the Saluva dynasty, feudatories of the Vijayanagara Empire, between roughly 1409 and 1610 CE. Within this tradition the temple is valued less for scale than for the rarity of its design, since four-faced basadis allowing worship from every direction are uncommon even among Karnataka's extensive network of Digambara shrines, giving Gerusoppa's example particular significance among scholars of Jain temple architecture.

Inscriptional and architectural evidence places the temple's construction in 1562, during the reign of Rani Chennabhairadevi, who ruled Gerusoppa through its most prosperous era and was known to European traders of the time as the "Pepper Queen" for her control of the regional spice trade. Built in grey granite in the Vijayanagara idiom, the shrine rests on a star-shaped platform reached by steep stone stairways cut directly into rock, with each of its four matching doorways opening onto a shared garbhagriha, or sanctum, at the structure's centre.

The sanctum houses four seated Tirthankara images facing outward through the four entrances: Rishabhanatha, Ajitanatha, Sambhavanatha and Abhinandananatha, the first four Jinas in the Jain lineage, each accessible to worshippers approaching from a different compass direction without need to circle the shrine. This arrangement reflects accounts that Gerusoppa once supported as many as 108 Jain temples spread across some 700 acres under royal and merchant patronage, a scale of religious building that marked the town as one of the major Digambara centres of the Kanara coast before its decline following the Portuguese raids and subsequent disorder of the early seventeenth century.

Of that extensive temple complex only a handful of structures survive at the site today, with the Chaturmukha Basadi recognised as the best preserved and most frequently documented, placing it alongside the ruins of Gerusoppa's fort and the Chandranatha basadi at nearby Bhatkal as key reference points for historians tracing the Saluva dynasty's architectural legacy. The monument is now maintained as a protected heritage site drawing researchers and pilgrims to Honnavar taluk, and its postal recognition through the Gersoppa Jyothinagar post office cancellation keeps the temple's distinctive four-faced design in circulation as a marker of the district's Jain past.
