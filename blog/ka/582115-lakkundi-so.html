Brahma Jinalaya

ಬ್ರಹ್ಮ ಜಿನಾಲಯ

The temple's name traces to an unusual iconographic choice inside its inner mandapa, where sculptures of Chaturmukha Brahma, the four-faced creator deity, and the goddess Saraswati were installed alongside the expected Tirthankara images, giving the shrine its distinctive title of Brahma Jinalaya rather than a name drawn from a single Jina. Local tradition and inscriptional record attribute its founding to Attimabbe, also recorded as Attiyabbe and honoured as Danacintamani Attimbbe, the wife of the Chalukya governor Dandanayaka Nagadeva, who is said to have secured permission from the Chalukya king Satyashraya to raise a Jaina temple at Lakkundi in the early eleventh century.

Dated by inscription to around 1007 CE, Brahma Jinalaya is reckoned the oldest surviving temple at Lakkundi and stands among the most refined examples of Kalyana Chalukya, or Western Chalukya, religious architecture in South India. Dedicated principally to the Tirthankara Neminatha, it belongs to the broader Jain basadi tradition that flourished alongside Shaiva and Vaishnava shrines in the town, reflecting a period of religious plurality under Chalukya patronage. Attimabbe herself is remembered beyond this single commission as a major patron of Kannada literature and Jain dharma, celebrated in later tradition as a sponsor of numerous basadis and of copies of Jain scriptural texts, which lends the temple significance as a marker of lay patronage alongside royal endowment.

The temple faces east and follows a trikutachala-influenced plan with a mukhamandapa, a gudhamandapa, and a sanctum crowned by a stepped vimana superstructure; reported dimensions place the structure at roughly 93 feet in length and 35 feet in width, with a sikhara rising about 42 feet in a tiered, chaturasra form topped by a sukhanasi projection. Built predominantly of chlorite schist, commonly called soapstone, worked alongside sandstone, the temple displays the lathe-turned, intricately carved pillars characteristic of Chalukyan workshops, along with a richly ornamented doorway to the garbhagriha, features that architectural historians cite as forerunners of carving techniques later refined under the Hoysalas.

The temple suffered considerable damage during the political upheavals that affected the Deccan in the thirteenth century and after, including the defacement of sculptures; a Mahavira image from the complex was found decapitated, and when British-era surveyors under Henry Cousens documented the site in the nineteenth century, they described finding the shrine in a neglected and filthy state, serving at the time as a roost for bats. That account, preserved in Archaeological Survey records, marks an important turning point in the temple's modern history, since it prompted the clearance and documentation that brought Brahma Jinalaya back into scholarly and conservation attention after centuries of disuse.

Today the temple is a centrally protected monument administered by the Archaeological Survey of India, which maintains the structure along with the adjoining stepped well, or kalyani, and the cluster of Chalukyan-era temples scattered across Lakkundi, a town once recorded as having held more than fifty shrines. Lakkundi has in recent years drawn renewed conservation interest, including efforts tied to its consideration for UNESCO World Heritage recognition as part of the broader Chalukyan monuments of the Deccan, and the ASI site museum near the temple now displays sculptural fragments recovered from Lakkundi's ruined structures. The Brahma Jinalaya is accordingly visited less as an active place of Jain worship today than as a protected archaeological monument, studied by scholars and heritage tourists for its place in the architectural lineage running from Chalukya to Hoysala craftsmanship.
