ISRO Radar

Sriharikota island, a narrow, spindle-shaped strip of land on the Bay of Bengal coast in Andhra Pradesh, was surveyed and selected in 1969 as the site for India's satellite launching station, chiefly for its equatorial proximity and its clear eastward trajectory out over open water, which kept populated areas safely out of range of falling rocket stages. The facility began operations on 9 October 1971 under the name Sriharikota Range, abbreviated SHAR, and was renamed on 5 September 2002 in honour of Satish Dhawan, the aerospace scientist and former ISRO chairman credited with steering the organisation through its formative decades, becoming the Satish Dhawan Space Centre while retaining the familiar SDSC-SHAR acronym.

As the primary spaceport of the Indian Space Research Organisation, the centre anchors nearly every orbital mission the country has flown, including the PSLV and GSLV launch vehicle programmes, interplanetary missions such as the Mars Orbiter Mission, and the Chandrayaan lunar missions. Its significance rests less on any single structure than on its role as the operational nerve centre of India's launch infrastructure, encompassing launch pads, solid propellant plants, vehicle assembly buildings, and the range safety and tracking apparatus that every mission depends on from liftoff through orbital insertion, making it the institutional counterpart to the temples and civic landmarks more commonly associated with postal cancellations.

The radar and tracking network referred to in postal and popular usage as the ISRO radar is built around precision monopulse tracking radars that have been operational at the centre since 2009, supplemented by the indigenously developed Multi-Object Tracking Radar, commissioned in the following decade after a roughly thirty-month development effort costing around Rs 245 crore and capable of simultaneously tracking around ten objects during ascent. Range operations as a whole draw on a network of several radars working alongside telemetry and command stations and a mission control centre, a system whose early groundwork was laid in the 1970s by scientists who established SHAR's original telemetry, tracking and instrumentation capability well before the first PSLV flights.

A defining event for this tracking infrastructure came on 18 July 1980, when the SLV-3 rocket carrying the Rohini satellite achieved India's first successful indigenous orbital launch from Sriharikota, making the country the seventh in the world to reach orbit on its own vehicle; the radar and telemetry systems that monitored that flight were the direct ancestors of the far more capable network now guiding PSLV and GSLV missions. The same tracking chain played a visible role during the final, tension-filled minutes of the Chandrayaan-3 lunar landing in August 2023, when ground stations at Sriharikota and elsewhere fed the real-time telemetry that confirmed the lander's descent and touchdown.

Today the centre's tracking and range safety systems are operated and continually upgraded by ISRO in coordination with the Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre and other agency units responsible for radar and electronics development, with newer indigenous radars progressively replacing older imported systems as launch cadence has increased. The post office at Sriharikota Range, serving the township attached to the spaceport in Tirupati district, sits at the edge of a facility that functions less as a monument to be visited than as working infrastructure, quietly tracking rockets through every Indian launch while remaining largely closed to the public outside scheduled visit programmes.
