Sidi Saiyad’s Mosque Window

સીદી સૈયદની મસ્જિદની જાળી

The mosque takes its name from Sidi Saiyyed (also rendered Sidi Sayyid), an Abyssinian-born noble recorded on a marble tablet within the building as Shaikh Sa'id al-Habshi Sultani. He had reportedly arrived in Gujarat as a Habshi retainer of Rumi Khan, a Turkish military commander in the service of the Sultanate, before rising to court rank himself. The mosque he commissioned in 980 AH, corresponding to 1572-73 CE, was completed in the very last year before the Mughal conquest of Gujarat under Akbar ended the independent Sultanate, making it one of the final monuments of that architectural tradition before it was absorbed into Mughal patronage.

As a building type the mosque belongs to the broader corpus of Gujarat Sultanate mosque architecture, an Indo-Islamic idiom that fused Islamic liturgical requirements with the stone-carving vocabulary of regional Hindu and Jain craftsmen. Its significance rests less on its role as a congregational mosque, which remains modest, than on its status as the terminal masterpiece of that nearly century-and-a-half-long building tradition. The carved jaali screens it houses have since been informally adopted as a visual emblem of Ahmedabad itself, and their abstracted form was taken up as the official logo of the Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad after the institute's founding in 1961, giving a sixteenth-century devotional structure a continuing institutional afterlife far beyond its original religious purpose.

Constructed from yellow sandstone, the mosque is a wholly arcuated structure without the large domes typical of grander Sultanate mosques, its ten carved stone jaali windows set into the side and rear arches forming its principal architectural statement. Each screen is cut from a single stone slab and depicts paired trees, usually a central tree with a thick trunk whose branches, tendrils, leaves and floral offshoots spread across the frame, flanking a stylized palm with fanned leaves at its crown. The most celebrated of the ten, popularly called the Tree of Life jaali, achieves an unusually delicate interlacing of carved stone branches that has made it a byword for the technical sophistication Gujarati stone-carvers reached in the final decades of the Sultanate period.

What distinguishes this particular window from the mosque's other nine screens, which are themselves finely worked, is the sheer structural daring of its design: the branches cross and recross one another without any supporting lattice grid behind them, relying purely on the carved stone's own continuity for strength. This has led art historians and conservators to single it out repeatedly as the apex of Gujarati jaali work, reproduced on everything from museum postcards to tourism posters, and it is this specific screen rather than the mosque as a whole that draws most visitors and photographers to the site, many of whom spend little time elsewhere in the small complex.

The mosque continues to function as an active place of worship and is separately listed as a protected heritage structure under the Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation's heritage listings, which has kept its jaali screens under conservation oversight even as the surrounding Lal Darwaza area has grown into a dense commercial district. The IIM Ahmedabad archives document the institute's own adoption of the motif, including the design process by which the carved tree was abstracted into the geometric logo still used on its official seal and publications today. Positioned at the edge of the old walled city near Ahmedabad's municipal corporation headquarters, the mosque is maintained today as a working shrine that also operates, almost incidentally, as one of the city's most visited single-room galleries of stone craftsmanship.
