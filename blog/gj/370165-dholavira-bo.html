Harappan Culture

Dholavira takes its name from the present-day village in Kachchh district, Gujarat, near which the ruins stand; the ancient Harappan settlement's original name remains unknown, as the Indus script has never been deciphered. The site was first brought to notice in the early 1960s by a local resident, Shambhudan Gadhvi, who alerted authorities to the mound, and it was formally identified in 1967-68 by J. P. Joshi of the Archaeological Survey of India. Local tradition in the surrounding Khadir island refers to the ruins informally as Kotada, meaning 'large fort,' reflecting the imposing scale of the stone fortifications still visible above ground before systematic excavation began.

As one of the five largest Harappan settlements and the fifth largest among the eight major cities of the Indus Valley Civilisation, Dholavira occupies a distinctive place in the archaeology of Bronze Age South Asia. Positioned on Khadir Bet, an island within the seasonally flooded Rann of Kachchh, it bridged trade routes linking Sindh, Gujarat's coastline, and peninsular India, and its material culture spans the Early, Mature, and Late Harappan phases. Its inscription with UNESCO in 2021 made it the first Indus Valley Civilisation property in India to receive World Heritage status, recognising the settlement's exceptional testimony to an urban tradition that flourished and declined between roughly 3000 and 1500 BCE.

Excavated across thirteen field seasons between 1989 and 2005 under archaeologist R. S. Bisht of the ASI, Dholavira revealed a tripartite layout of citadel, middle town, and lower town enclosed within massive stone ramparts, a departure from the fired-brick construction dominant at sites such as Mohenjo-daro and Harappa. The city's planners cut at least sixteen reservoirs directly into bedrock and hillside, some roughly seven metres deep and around seventy metres long, channelling monsoon runoff from two seasonal streams through an elaborate network of drains and sluices. This stone-based hydraulic engineering, combined with stepwells and a centrally located great bath on raised ground, allowed a substantial population to persist in one of the most arid parts of the subcontinent for close to a millennium and a half.

Among the finds that distinguish Dholavira is a signboard of ten large gypsum-inlaid characters in the Indus script, discovered near the northern gateway of the citadel and believed to have been mounted on a wooden board above the entrance; it remains among the longest Indus inscriptions yet recovered, though the script itself has never been read. Excavators also uncovered a stadium-like ceremonial ground, multiple burial structures including some without skeletal remains, and evidence of bead-making, shell and copper working, indicating a city with specialised craft industries alongside its administrative and defensive functions. The castle and bailey arrangement of the citadel, with its own fortification distinct from the middle and lower towns, points to a stratified social and political order managing the settlement.

The site continues to be maintained by the Archaeological Survey of India, whose on-site museum at Dholavira displays pottery, seals, beads, copper objects, and architectural fragments recovered during the excavations, giving visitors context for the exposed ruins nearby. Following the 2021 UNESCO listing, the Gujarat state government and ASI have expanded visitor infrastructure around Khadir Bet, including signage, pathways, and conservation work on the exposed stone walls and reservoirs to limit erosion from seasonal flooding of the Rann. Today Dholavira functions both as a protected monument under active archaeological study and as a heritage tourism destination reached via the causeway connecting Khadir island to the Gujarat mainland.
