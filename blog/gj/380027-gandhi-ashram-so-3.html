Sabarmati Ashram

સાબરમતી આશ્રમ

Gandhi's ashram on the Sabarmati took shape in two stages rather than a single founding act. He first established the Satyagraha Ashram at Kochrab, a village near Ahmedabad, on 25 May 1915, soon after returning from South Africa, naming it for the creed of truth-force he had developed there. An outbreak of plague in Kochrab in 1917 forced a search for a larger, more open site, and Gandhi purchased roughly thirty-six acres of barren land on the river's western bank, placing ownership in a collective trust rather than in his own name, a detail consistent with the community's self-governing character from the outset.

The ashram's significance rests on its dual identity as both a religious community and a political nerve centre, a combination Gandhi intended deliberately. He envisioned it as a fellowship bound by vows of truth, non-violence, celibacy, non-possession and bread-labour, while simultaneously functioning as a training ground for workers who would carry the campaign for self-rule into villages and cities across the country. This fusion of ashram discipline with organised political resistance gave Sabarmati Ashram a status distinct from other contemporary religious retreats, and it is recognised today as one of the handful of sites most directly tied to the formation of Gandhian thought and method.

Between 1917 and 1930, the settlement grew from a cluster of modest structures into a working campus of roughly sixty residential and institutional buildings, housing a school, a weaving unit, a dairy, and quarters for a community that at various points included several hundred residents and volunteers, including the Dalit family Gandhi controversially admitted in 1918, which tested the ashram's professed commitment to ending untouchability. Hriday Kunj, his own plain cottage, became the practical administrative hub, built in the austere, unornamented style that characterised the whole complex, with construction and layout overseen by the Satyagraha Ashram's own trust rather than any professional architectural commission.

The ashram's best-known single event remains Gandhi's departure from its gates on 12 March 1930, when he set out with a small band of followers on the 240-kilometre march to Dandi to break the salt laws, swearing he would not return to Sabarmati until swaraj was won. That vow permanently altered the site's use: the ashram was handed over for other purposes in the years that followed and was never again Gandhi's settled residence, so its subsequent life has been almost entirely as a memorial rather than as a functioning commune, a shift that sets it apart from ashrams that have continued in uninterrupted religious use.

The campus is now administered by the Sabarmati Ashram Preservation and Memorial Trust, which maintains Hriday Kunj, the Gandhi Smarak Sangrahalaya museum, an archive of letters and photographs, and a library, and since 2021 the site has also been the subject of a government-backed redevelopment plan, designed by architect Bimal Patel, that proposes expanding the protected precinct from about five acres to fifty-five while leaving Hriday Kunj, its museum and Magan Villa structurally untouched. The plan has drawn public objections, including a legal challenge from Gandhi's great-grandson Tushar Gandhi that the Gujarat High Court later dismissed, so the ashram today functions simultaneously as a settled heritage museum and as the subject of an active, contested planning process over how much of its surrounding campus should change.
