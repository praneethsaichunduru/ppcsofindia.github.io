Lakshman Jhula

लक्ष्मण झूला

According to tradition recorded in the Ramayana, Lakshman, the younger brother of Lord Rama, crossed the Ganges at this very point on a bridge of jute rope during the period of exile, lending the crossing its name. In keeping with this legend, a jute suspension bridge was first erected at the site in 1889 to honour that memory, allowing pilgrims and villagers to pass over the river where the epic event was believed to have occurred. That original rope structure proved vulnerable to the river's seasonal fury and was swept away in the floods of 1924, leaving the crossing in need of a sturdier replacement that could withstand the Ganges in spate.

Lakshman Jhula belongs to the category of historic engineering landmarks fused with religious pilgrimage geography, and its significance rests on both counts. As a physical crossing, it linked the Tehri Garhwal bank of the Ganges to the town of Rishikesh, long serving pedestrians, sadhus, and two-wheeler riders where no comparable bridge existed nearby. As a religious site, it is treated as hallowed ground associated with the Ramayana narrative, drawing pilgrims who visit less for the ironwork than for the belief that this stretch of river once bore Lakshman's footsteps. This dual identity, part infrastructure and part tirtha, made it one of the defining reference points of Rishikesh's sacred topography for nearly a century.

Following the loss of the jute bridge, the United Provinces Public Works Department undertook construction of a permanent iron suspension bridge at the site between 1927 and 1929, and the structure was formally opened to traffic on 11 April 1930. Spanning roughly 450 feet across the Ganges and suspended about 70 feet above the water, the bridge connected the settlements of Tapovan and Jonk on either bank. Built almost entirely of iron with a cantilevered suspension design typical of early twentieth-century colonial-era river crossings, it was engineered to carry foot traffic and light two-wheeler movement rather than motor vehicles, a limitation that shaped how the surrounding town developed around it over the following decades.

The bridge is flanked by a cluster of ashrams, temples, and riverside ghats that grew up around it, most notably the thirteen-storey Trayambakeshwar Temple standing near its eastern approach, which became a visual counterpart to the span itself in countless depictions of Rishikesh. By the latter half of the twentieth century, the combination of heavy foot traffic, overloaded two-wheelers, and decades of exposure to the Ganges's moisture had placed considerable strain on the ageing ironwork. A Public Works Department inspection eventually found the structure's load-bearing capacity critically compromised, and on 7 July 2019, after nearly ninety years of continuous use, the bridge was closed to all traffic on safety grounds, ending its functional life as a river crossing even as it remained standing.

Rather than repairing the original span, the Uttarakhand government opted to build a replacement nearby, the Bajrang Setu, a cable suspension bridge roughly 132 metres long with glass-floored pedestrian walkways flanking a central two-wheeler deck, constructed at a cost of about sixty crore rupees and targeted for completion around the end of 2025; in the interim, a temporary ferry service has carried foot traffic across the Ganges at the site. The old iron bridge itself remains in place, closed to the public and awaiting a decision on its long-term conservation even as the new crossing nears completion. For now, Lakshman Jhula persists chiefly as a photographed backdrop and a point of reference for navigating the ashrams and markets of Rishikesh, a retired structure that visitors still gather around rather than cross.
