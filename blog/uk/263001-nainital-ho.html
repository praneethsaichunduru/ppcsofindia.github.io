Naina Devi Temple

नैना देवी मंदिर

According to prevailing Shakta tradition, Naina Devi Temple owes both its name and its sanctity to an episode from the Daksha Yajna legend, in which the grief-stricken Shiva carried the corpse of his wife Sati across the cosmos while Vishnu's discus cut her body into pieces that fell at scattered points across the subcontinent. The spot where her eyes, or "nain", are said to have fallen became known as Nain-tal, the lake of the eye, and the shrine that rose on its northern shore took the name Naina Devi. This etymological link between the goddess's eyes, the crescent-shaped lake below, and the hill station that grew around it is still the explanation given locally for how both the temple and the town acquired their names.

The temple is counted among the 51 Shakti Peethas, the network of shrines across South Asia held to mark the sites where parts of Sati's body fell, placing Naina Devi within the same ritual category as better-known Peethas such as Kamakhya and Kalighat. Within Uttarakhand it functions as the presiding deity shrine of Nainital town, drawing pilgrims from across the Kumaon region and beyond rather than serving only a local congregation. Its dual identity as both a major pilgrimage destination and a lakeside landmark has made it one of the handful of sites in the hill station treated as essential to any visit, religious or otherwise, and it remains an active, daily-functioning temple rather than a purely historical monument.

The original Naina Devi temple stood somewhat differently sited until a catastrophic landslide struck Nainital on 18 September 1880, after roughly 33 inches of rain fell over the preceding two days and a minor earthquake loosened the slope above the Victoria Hotel; the slip buried the hotel, the Assembly Rooms and the temple itself, killing more than 150 people, many of them workers and soldiers who had been sent to the site. The shrine was rebuilt close to its original location on the lake's northern shore within a few years of the disaster, and in its present form the sanctum houses a pair of eyes symbolising the goddess, flanked by an image of Kali to one side and Ganesha to the other, with a Hanuman shrine near the entrance and a peepal tree within the complex; two stone lions, the goddess's traditional vehicle, stand along the approach.

The temple's heaviest footfall occurs during Navratri, when the nine-day festival draws large numbers of pilgrims for daily aarti and special decorations of the deity, but Nainital's Kumaon region also observes the related Nanda Devi Mela each September, a week-long fair culminating in a large procession that links temple worship with the town's civic calendar. The combination of the 1880 disaster's death toll and the subsequent rebuilding has itself become part of local memory, cited in municipal and tourism accounts of Nainital as the reason the temple now stands slightly apart from the exact site of the original structure, overlooking the lake rather than directly beside its edge as before.

Present-day upkeep of the temple falls to local temple management authorities working alongside Nainital's municipal administration, which coordinates crowd arrangements and security during the Navratri and Nanda Devi Mela periods when visitor numbers rise sharply; state tourism bodies also list the shrine as a key stop in promotional material for the Kumaon circuit. The temple today draws a steady mix of religious pilgrims completing the Shakti Peetha circuit and general visitors to Nainital who combine the shrine visit with a walk along the Mall Road beside Naini Lake, making it one of the few sites in the hill station that functions simultaneously as an active place of worship and a fixture of the town's everyday tourist itinerary.
