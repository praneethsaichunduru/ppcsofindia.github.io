Mountains

पहाड़

The mountains of Uttarakhand carry no single founding legend, for they are counted among the oldest ranges on earth, thrust up over tens of millions of years as the Indian tectonic plate drove into Asia. Instead, their origin stories are mythological rather than geological: the Kumaon and Garhwal ranges were held in Hindu tradition to be the literal abode of the gods, with Nanda Devi herself worshipped as a patron goddess of both regions. The peaks' very names reflect this layering of belief and perception, from Nanda Devi, meaning the bliss-giving goddess, to Panchachuli, the five hearths, popularly said to be where the five Pandava brothers of the Mahabharata cooked their final meal before ascending to the heavens.

As part of the Central Himalayas, these ranges hold both ecological and spiritual distinction. Nanda Devi, at 7,816 metres, is the second-highest mountain in India and the highest located entirely within its borders, ranking 23rd in the world; the surrounding Nanda Devi National Park, notified in 1982, was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1988 and later folded into the wider Nanda Devi Biosphere Reserve, which joined UNESCO's World Network of Biosphere Reserves in 2004. The reserve, covering a core area of over 600 square kilometres alongside the adjoining Valley of Flowers National Park, protects one of the few remaining undisturbed habitats in the high Himalaya, while the broader ranges anchor the Char Dham and Panch Kedar pilgrimage circuits central to Hindu devotional geography.

Among the region's other major summits, Trishul, rising to 7,120 metres in Chamoli district with ridges extending into Kumaon, holds the distinction of being the first peak above 7,000 metres ever climbed, scaled by Tom Longstaff's expedition in 1907. Further east near Munsiyari in Pithoragarh district stands the Panchachuli massif, a cluster of five snow peaks ranging from roughly 6,334 to 6,904 metres; its highest point, Panchachuli II, was first summited by an Indo-Tibetan Border Police team led by Mahendra Singh in 1973. These mountains sustain glacial systems feeding the Ganga and its tributaries, and their slopes descend through a succession of ecological zones, from subtropical sal and pine forests to oak and rhododendron belts, alpine meadows, and permanent snowfields hosting species such as the snow leopard, musk deer, and bharal.

The inner sanctuary ringing Nanda Devi, hemmed by a nearly continuous wall of peaks over 6,000 metres, resisted exploration for decades; early attempts by W. W. Graham and others in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries failed to penetrate it, and it was only in 1934 that the British mountaineers Eric Shipton and H. W. Tilman forced a route through the Rishi Ganga gorge, opening the sanctuary to subsequent expeditions and eventually to the first ascent of Nanda Devi itself in 1936 by an Anglo-American team including Noel Odell and H. W. Tilman. Concerns over ecological degradation from mountaineering traffic later led Indian authorities to close the inner sanctuary to climbers in 1983, a restriction that remains largely in force and has allowed the core habitat to recover with minimal human disturbance.

Today the Nanda Devi Biosphere Reserve is jointly managed by Uttarakhand's forest department and national park authorities, who conduct periodic ecological monitoring and restrict access to a buffer zone while the core sanctuary stays closed to trekkers and climbers alike. The wider Kumaon and Garhwal ranges, meanwhile, continue to draw pilgrims to the Char Dham shrines and mountaineers to peaks such as Trishul and the Panchachuli group, sustaining a tourism economy that forms one of the principal livelihoods for towns like Nainital, Munsiyari, and Joshimath at the ranges' foothills.
