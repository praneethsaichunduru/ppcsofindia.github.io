St. Thomas Church

സെന്റ് തോമസ് പള്ളി

Tradition holds that the Apostle Thomas, who is said to have landed at Kodungallur on the Malabar coast around 52 CE, returned to the region roughly a decade later and travelled inland along the Periyar river to Malayattoor. There, facing hostility from local inhabitants, he is said to have retreated up the hill overlooking the river and remained there in prayer, leaving behind an impression on the rock that devotees still venerate as his footprint. A further strand of the oral tradition recounts that as he prayed, a golden cross miraculously appeared on the stone at the summit, a spot known locally as Kurishumudi, meaning 'hill of the cross,' which gives the hilltop shrine its name and its enduring pull for pilgrims.

Malayattoor is counted among the seven and a half churches traditionally attributed to St. Thomas's ministry in Kerala, and it ranks as one of the foremost Marian and apostolic pilgrimage centres of the Syro-Malabar Catholic Church. The parish church itself is reckoned among the oldest Christian establishments on the Malabar coast, with local records tracing an organised congregation at the site back to around the ninth century, long before the present structure took shape. In recognition of its standing, the church was raised to the status of an archdiocesan pilgrim centre by the Syro-Malabar church authorities in 1998, and it is today formally designated an international shrine, one of only a small number of such apostolic shrines recognised worldwide.

The church that stands on the riverbank below the hill has been rebuilt and enlarged repeatedly as the pilgrimage gained prominence, with the present sanctuary reflecting a largely twentieth-century reconstruction built to accommodate growing congregations, while retaining elements and relics tied to its earlier incarnations. A separate chapel marks the hilltop site of the footprint and cross, reached by a steep forest path of several hundred steps cut into the hillside above the Periyar, a climb that forms the physical core of the pilgrimage experience. The sanctuary complex also incorporates a museum and facilities built up over successive renovation phases funded through diocesan and public contributions, reflecting the scale of infrastructure now needed to manage the volume of annual visitors.

The defining event of the calendar is the Malayattoor Perunnal, held over roughly ten days culminating on the Sunday following Easter, during which pilgrims converge on the hill chanting invocations to 'Ponninkurishu Muthappan,' the Apostle venerated under the sign of the golden cross. Many undertake the ascent barefoot, and a visible number carry heavy wooden crosses on their shoulders the entire way up as an act of penance, a practice that has become the most recognisable image associated with the shrine and is widely photographed and reported on during the festival season. The event draws participants not only from Kerala's various Christian denominations but also, notably, from other faith communities in the surrounding region, reflecting the site's broader local reputation beyond its specifically Christian significance.

Attendance at the Perunnal has grown substantially in recent decades, with diocesan estimates in recent years placing the number of pilgrims climbing the hill during the festival period at several hundred thousand, necessitating coordinated crowd-management and infrastructure arrangements involving the local administration, police, and forest department given the hill's location within a reserved forest area. The shrine is administered under the Archdiocese of Ernakulam-Angamaly, which oversees both the riverside parish church and the hilltop chapel as a single pilgrim complex. Today the site functions year-round as a destination for individual pilgrims and organised groups alike, with the Perunnal remaining its single largest observance and the steep climb to Kurishumudi continuing to serve as the practical and symbolic centre of the visit.
