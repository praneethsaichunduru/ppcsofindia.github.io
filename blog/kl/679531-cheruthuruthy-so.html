Vesham in Kathakali

കഥകളിയിലെ വേഷം

The term vesham, meaning "guise" or "disguise" in Malayalam, refers to the elaborate character embodiment at the heart of Kathakali, Kerala's classical dance-drama. Rather than relying on dialogue, Kathakali performers communicate character and emotion through a codified system of facial makeup, costume, and headgear, each combination signalling a specific moral or dramatic type before a single gesture is made. This visual grammar evolved over generations of temple and court performance as the art form matured from its sixteenth and seventeenth century antecedents, Krishnanattam and Ramanattam, into a distinct theatrical tradition. The vesham system allowed audiences, often watching all-night performances under oil lamps, to read a character's nature instantly from colour and form alone.

Kathakali recognises five principal vesham categories: pacha, kathi, thadi, kari, and minukku, each tied to a particular moral register. Pacha, a green-faced vesham, denotes noble and divine heroes such as Krishna and Rama. Kathi, or "knife" vesham, marks characters of royal birth who are nonetheless arrogant or villainous, identified by an upturned, blade-like red-and-white pattern across the cheek, as seen in Ravana. Thadi, the bearded category, divides further into red beard for violently evil figures, white beard for elevated beings such as Hanuman, and black beard for forest-dwelling hunters. Kari vesham, with its jet-black face dotted in red and white, portrays demonesses and gruesome figures, while minukku, a softly shaded yellow or orange-toned face, is reserved for sages, women, and gentle characters. This classification system forms the core identity of Kathakali as a performance art and is central to its recognition as one of India's major classical dance forms.

The physical preparation of a vesham is itself a demanding craft, traditionally undertaken by specialist makeup artists called chutty karan using pigments derived from rice paste, lime, turmeric, indigo, and soot, built up in layers over several hours before a performance. The most distinctive feature, the chutty, is a white, paper-like ridge of rice-paste and lime built along the jawline of pacha, kathi, and certain thadi characters, which frames the face and exaggerates expressions visible from a distance. Elaborate crowns, broad skirts stiffened with layers of cloth, and oversized eye makeup using seed-derived black kohl further extend the transformation, allowing performers to sustain the intense eye and eyebrow movements, or navarasa expressions, central to Kathakali's acting technique. Training to execute these vesham roles convincingly typically spans six to eight years of rigorous physical and gestural instruction.

A defining institutional moment for the systematic teaching of vesham and Kathakali more broadly came with the founding of Kerala Kalamandalam in 1930 by the poet Vallathol Narayana Menon, working with his associate Manakkulam Mukundaraja and under the patronage of Kakkad Karanavappad. The institution first took shape at Kunnamkulam before relocating by 1936 to its permanent riverside campus on the banks of the Bharathapuzha at Cheruthuruthy, a site chosen for its tranquillity suited to disciplined training. At a time when royal and temple patronage for performing arts was declining sharply, Kalamandalam became the first formal school in Kerala to train students systematically in Kathakali, including its exacting vesham disciplines, rather than leaving transmission to informal apprenticeship under individual masters.

Kerala Kalamandalam today functions as a deemed-to-be university for art and culture, offering structured degree and diploma programmes in Kathakali, Mohiniyattam, and other Kerala art forms, and continues to train the chutty specialists responsible for vesham makeup alongside performers themselves. The institution regularly stages Kathakali performances for visitors at its Cheruthuruthy campus, giving the public direct access to the pacha, kathi, thadi, kari, and minukku veshams described in classical treatises on the art. Administered under the Kerala state government's department of culture, Kalamandalam has been joined by other training centres across the state, but it retains its founding role as the principal reference institution through which the vesham tradition is formally taught and certified today.
