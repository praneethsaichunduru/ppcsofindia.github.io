Indian Bison (Gaur)

ഇന്ത്യൻ കാട്ടുപോത്ത് (ഗൗർ)

The name "gaur" derives from Sanskrit-rooted vernacular terms used across the Indian subcontinent for wild cattle, while the species carries the scientific name Bos gaurus, distinguishing it from domesticated cattle and from the semi-domesticated gayal found in parts of Northeast India. Within Kerala and across much of its range it is popularly called the Indian bison, though it is not a true bison at all but a wild ox, more closely related to domestic cattle than to the American or European bison with which early European naturalists and hunters confused it. The misleading common name has nonetheless persisted in everyday usage, including on signage and literature associated with reserves such as Parambikulam, where the animal remains one of the most frequently sighted large mammals.

As the largest extant species of wild cattle in the world, the gaur holds considerable ecological significance as a keystone herbivore in the forest ecosystems of the Western Ghats, Central India, and parts of Southeast Asia. It is classified as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, with its global population estimated at under 20,000 mature individuals and declining in several range states, making strongholds like Parambikulam Tiger Reserve disproportionately important to the species' long-term survival. In India it receives the highest legal protection under Schedule I of the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, and its presence is often used by wildlife managers as an indicator of forest health, since the species requires a mosaic of dense cover and open grassland to thrive.

Adult gaur are physically imposing animals, with bulls standing up to roughly 220 centimetres at the shoulder and weighing as much as 1,500 kilograms, making them the heaviest of all wild bovids and the only ones to regularly exceed two metres in shoulder height. Both sexes carry curved horns, pale yellowish at the base and darkening toward the tips, while a muscular ridge along the back and a pronounced dewlap beneath the chin give mature bulls a distinctively powerful silhouette. Within Parambikulam, gaur are sustained by a landscape shaped since the early twentieth century by scientific forestry, including one of the earliest systematically managed teak plantations in the world, interspersed with natural moist-deciduous and semi-evergreen forest that together provide the grazing and browsing habitat the species depends on.

A particularly notable feature of the Parambikulam landscape is the Kannimara teak tree, estimated to be roughly 350 years old, standing about 40 metres tall with a girth exceeding 6 metres, and recognised with the Government of India's Mahavriksha Puraskar as one of the country's great heritage trees; it stands within the same forest mosaic that gaur herds traverse while grazing in the reserve's grasslands. Visitors travelling along the reserve's safari routes and nature trails frequently encounter gaur herds grazing in open clearings during early morning and late afternoon hours, often alongside elephants and occasionally under the watch of the reserve's resident tiger population, making sightings here a defining experience of a Parambikulam visit distinct from many other Western Ghats reserves.

Parambikulam Tiger Reserve, formally constituted in 2009-2010 after decades as a wildlife sanctuary dating to 1973, is administered by the Kerala Forest Department and spans roughly 644 square kilometres across Palakkad and Thrissur districts, forming part of a contiguous forest corridor linking with Tamil Nadu's Anamalai Tiger Reserve to its south. Indigenous communities, including the Kadar, Malasar, Muduvar, and Mala Malasar tribes, continue to reside within the reserve and are increasingly engaged as guides and guards in its ecotourism and conservation programmes, which combine anti-poaching patrols with managed grassland burning to sustain gaur forage. The reserve's periodic wildlife censuses, conducted jointly with neighbouring Tamil Nadu forest authorities, now track gaur numbers alongside tigers and elephants as part of a shared cross-border conservation effort for the Anamalai-Parambikulam landscape.
