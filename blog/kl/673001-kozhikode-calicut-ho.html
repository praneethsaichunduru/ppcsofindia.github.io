Mask of Theyyam

തെയ്യത്തിന്റെ മുഖംമൂടി

The term Theyyam is widely understood to be a Malayalam corruption of "Daivam," the Sanskrit-derived word for god, and the ritual is accordingly known in different localities as Kaliyattam or Thirayattam, the latter drawn from "thira," meaning village, since every settlement in North Malabar traditionally staged the performance at its own local shrine or sacred grove, called a kavu. Scholars trace its roots to ancient Dravidian traditions of ancestor and spirit worship among the Velan community, a lineage of practice that is believed to have evolved continuously over some fifteen hundred years. A recurring account credits the tantric figure Manakkadan Gurukul with demonstrating thirty-nine theyyam forms before Chirakkal Tampuran of the Kolathiri dynasty in Kannur, of which thirty-five were selected and consecrated, laying the template for the performances still staged today.

The painted mask and face, known as mukhathezhuthu, is the visual centerpiece through which a human performer is ritually transformed into a deity, ancestral spirit, or local hero for the duration of the kaliyattam. Theyyam is performed almost exclusively by men from Scheduled Caste communities such as the Malayan, Vannan, and Velan, who for the span of the ritual are treated as the living embodiment of the god and receive reverence, including from upper-caste worshippers, in a temporary inversion of everyday caste hierarchy. More than four hundred and fifty distinct theyyam forms have been documented across North Malabar, each tied to a specific deity, a particular narrative, a set costume, and a fixed sequence of invocation, music, and dance performed before the kavu or ancestral tharavadu house.

The mukhathezhuthu is built up entirely from natural pigments rather than manufactured paint: rice paste supplies the white, turmeric and a turmeric-lime mixture produce yellow and red, and black is made by burning paddy husks to ash and binding it with coconut oil, with additional tones drawn from saffron powder and quicklime. Applying a single elaborate facial design can take between eight and twelve hours, carried out by a specialist painter who works strictly within an inherited pattern specific to that deity, since personal improvisation is traditionally forbidden. The finished mask-like face, often extended by a towering headdress of arecanut sheath, coconut fronds, or bamboo, is paired with a vividly colored costume, and together these elements are intended not as decoration but as the visible sign that the wearer has ritually ceased to be himself for the duration of the performance.

Theyyam's performance calendar itself functions as a defining structural feature: the season opens on the tenth day of the Malayalam month of Thulam, around October, and continues for roughly seven months until the middle of Edavam in late May or June, after which the ritual cycle falls silent until the following year. The final performances of each season are traditionally held at Madayi Kavu and the Kalarivathukkal Bhagavathy Temple, both historically linked to the Kolathiri royal family, underscoring the ritual's long association with the former princely house of Kolathunadu that once ruled much of North Malabar. Kozhikode, though more closely tied in popular memory to the rival Zamorin dynasty and its maritime trade, lies within the broader cultural belt where Theyyam masks and performances remain an active, living practice rather than a historical relic confined to museums.

Contemporary scholarship and craft documentation, including efforts by institutions studying Kerala's intangible heritage and by artisan groups producing mukhathezhuthu-style decorative masks for wider audiences, have helped record the pigment recipes, headdress construction, and performance choreography that earlier survived only through oral transmission within performing families. Such mask forms are now also reproduced as collectible art objects and museum pieces, extending their reach well beyond the ritual kavus of North Malabar where they originated. In Kozhikode district the mask endures primarily as an emblem of this neighbouring ritual world, standing alongside the city's own medieval trading history as one of the two cultural threads most often used to introduce visitors to the layered identity of the Malabar coast.
