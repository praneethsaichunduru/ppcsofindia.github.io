Ponmudi Hill Resort

പൊന്മുടി ഹിൽ റിസോർട്ട്

Ponmudi, meaning "golden peak" in Malayalam, takes its name from the way the surrounding hills catch a golden hue at sunrise and sunset, a visual effect that gave the place its popular identity long before it developed into a resort town. Local tradition in the Peringamala area also links the hills to Parasurama, the mythical reclaimer of Kerala's coastal land, though this remains folk belief rather than recorded history. The area's documented past is tied more concretely to the colonial-era practice, common across the Western Ghats, of planting tea and cardamom on cleared hill slopes once the cool climate and terrain were recognised as suitable, a pattern that shaped much of Ponmudi's present landscape of plantations interspersed with forest.

Administratively part of Peringamala grama panchayat in Thiruvananthapuram district, Ponmudi sits at an elevation of roughly 1,100 metres in the Western Ghats, making it the capital region's principal hill resort and a counterpart, on a smaller scale, to better-known Kerala hill stations such as Munnar and Wayanad. Its significance is primarily recreational and ecological rather than historical or religious: it draws weekend and holiday visitors from Thiruvananthapuram city, roughly 60 kilometres away, while its forests fall within the broader Western Ghats biodiversity zone recognised as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, lending the destination a conservation dimension alongside its tourist appeal.

Access to Ponmudi is by a hill road built with numerous hairpin bends climbing through forest and plantation land, a route that remains the only vehicular approach and is itself considered one of the attractions for visitors. The Kerala Tourism Development Corporation operates the Golden Peak resort at Ponmudi, a property originally designed by the architect Laurie Baker and opened to the public in October 2009 with nine cottages and fourteen rooms; KTDC subsequently expanded the property in 2018 with fifteen additional cottages built at a cost of around Rs 3.2 crore. The surrounding reserve forest supports a deer park near the resort along with trekking trails leading to viewpoints such as the Golden Valley, where the Kallar river provides a picnic spot along the route to the erstwhile Upper Sanatorium built during the colonial period.

A notable recent development concerns connectivity rather than construction on the hill itself: the central government listed a proposed ropeway between Breimore, at the base of the ghat road, and Ponmudi under its Parvatmala ropeway scheme after Kerala first floated the idea in 2017, envisaging a cable-car span of roughly 3.6 kilometres to cut travel time up the winding road. The project illustrates both the pressure of growing visitor numbers on the narrow hill road and the state's continuing efforts to position Ponmudi as a more accessible counterpart to Kerala's larger hill-tourism circuits, even as implementation has moved slowly.

As of the mid-2020s, the ropeway project has stalled at the feasibility stage despite an initial budget allocation for a survey, with local reporting in 2025 noting that progress beyond a single coordination meeting has been limited, leaving the hairpin road as the sole route to the resort for the foreseeable future. Day-to-day tourism infrastructure, meanwhile, continues to be managed by KTDC alongside the Kerala Forest Department, which oversees the adjoining reserve forest and deer park. Ponmudi's draw remains practical rather than monumental: a short, manageable escape into Western Ghats forest and plantation country for residents of Thiruvananthapuram, sustained more by its cottages, trails and cool air than by any single landmark.
