College of Engineering Thiruvananthapuram

The College of Engineering Thiruvananthapuram (CET) traces its founding to 3 July 1939, when it was established under the patronage of Sree Chithira Thirunal Balarama Varma, the reigning Maharaja of the princely state of Travancore. It was set up as a constituent college of Travancore University at a time when the Travancore administration was pursuing an active policy of technical education, and it is recognised today as the oldest engineering institution in Kerala. The college began modestly, functioning out of the former office and bungalow of the state's Chief Engineer, with Major T. H. Mathewman, a British engineer, serving as its first Principal overseeing a small founding cohort of students.

As an institution, CET occupies a distinct place in South Indian technical education, having trained generations of engineers who went on to shape Kerala's and India's post-independence infrastructure, industry, and scientific establishments. Its early intake was limited to twenty-one students each in the degree and diploma streams across Civil, Mechanical, and Electrical Engineering, disciplines chosen to match Travancore's priorities in public works and utilities. Administrative control passed to the Government of Kerala following the creation of the state's Directorate of Technical Education in the late 1950s, and the college was later affiliated to the University of Kerala before becoming a constituent institution of the APJ Abdul Kalam Technological University in 2015, a shift from the Kerala Technological University arrangement noted in some earlier accounts.

In 1960 the college relocated from its original premises to its present campus at Kulathoor, roughly thirteen kilometres from Thiruvananthapuram city, on a sprawling site commonly cited at around 125 acres (roughly 101 hectares). The campus layout is distinctive: its main academic building is laid out in a plan that resembles a spanner when viewed from above, with departmental blocks, a central library, laboratories, workshops, hostels, staff quarters, a planetarium, and sports grounds arranged around it. Over the decades the college expanded well beyond its original three branches, and it now comprises eight full-fledged departments offering nine undergraduate programmes alongside numerous postgraduate, MBA, MCA, and doctoral programmes.

CET's alumni network is often cited as one of its defining strengths, having produced figures across engineering, public administration, and the arts. Among its most celebrated graduates is a group from the 1986 batch, sometimes referred to as the institution's contribution to India's space programme, several of whom rose to senior mission and directorial roles at the Indian Space Research Organisation, including positions tied to India's Mars and Moon missions. Other alumni have gone on to serve as senior state civil servants, including a former Accountant General of Kerala who graduated from the college's Civil Engineering department in 1970, reflecting the breadth of the institution's influence beyond purely technical careers.

The college today functions as a government engineering institution under the APJ Abdul Kalam Technological University system, continuing to admit students through Kerala's centralised engineering entrance process while maintaining postgraduate and doctoral research across its departments. Its planetarium and long-established workshops remain in regular use for both academic instruction and public outreach programmes organised by the institution. Having grown from a twenty-one-student department housed in a borrowed bungalow to a multi-disciplinary campus of national standing, CET is now commonly ranked among the leading government engineering colleges in South India and is commemorated through the post office that serves its campus, Thiruvananthapuram Engg College SO, in Kerala's postal network.
