Sree Padmanabhaswamy Temple

ശ്രീ പദ്മനാഭസ്വാമി ക്ഷേത്രം

The temple's founding is traced to a legend recorded in the Ananthashayana Mahatmya, concerning a sage named Divakara Muni, also known as Vilwamangalam Swamiyar. Vishnu is said to have appeared before him as a mischievous child and agreed to stay only on the condition that he never be scolded; when the sage eventually lost patience, the child vanished into a forest, with the sage following him to Ananthankadu, where Vishnu revealed himself in his reclining (Anantha Sayana) form inside an illam tree that collapsed into the shape of the deity. This episode is held to explain both the temple's location in Thiruvananthapuram, literally 'the city of the Sacred Serpent (Anantha),' and the deity's distinctive posture.

Sree Padmanabhaswamy Temple ranks among the foremost Vaishnavite shrines in India and is one of the 108 principal Divya Desams revered in Tamil Vaishnava tradition, placing it within a pan-South Indian network of sacred Vishnu sites dating to the early medieval period. Its significance is both devotional and dynastic: the temple has been the tutelary shrine of the Travancore royal house since at least the medieval period, and its records and inscriptions have long served historians as a source for the region's political and cultural history. The temple's reputed wealth, confirmed by a 21st-century court-ordered inventory, has additionally made it a reference point in discussions of historic Indian temple economies.

Although the site's worship traditions reach back centuries, with gopuram construction recorded from as early as 1566, the temple as it broadly appears today owes its granite structure, corridors, and the seven-tiered Pandyan-style gopuram largely to renovations carried out under Marthanda Varma in the 18th century. The sanctum houses an approximately 18-foot image of the reclining Vishnu, formed from an estimated 12,008 saligrama stones bound with a herbal and jaggery-based compound called 'katusharkara yogam,' and is viewed through three separate doors that frame, respectively, the deity's face, navel, and feet. On 19–20 January 1750, Marthanda Varma performed the Thrippadidanam ceremony, formally ceding the Travancore kingdom to the deity and adopting the title 'Padmanabha Dasa,' a designation retained symbolically by his successors.

The temple drew worldwide attention in 2011 when a Supreme Court-mandated inventory opened several of its sealed underground vaults, designated A through F by the court. Vaults opened in that exercise yielded gold coins, jewel-studded crowns, statues, and ceremonial objects valued by some estimates in the tens of billions of rupees' worth of gold and gems, among the largest such hoards ever documented at a single religious site. One vault, labelled 'B' and marked with a carved serpent emblem traditionally read as a warning against disturbance, was left unopened after the court stayed further action pending resolution of concerns raised about it, and it remains sealed.

Management of the temple was the subject of prolonged litigation after a 2011 Kerala High Court order sought to replace royal oversight with a state-appointed trust; on 13 July 2020, a two-judge Supreme Court bench of Justices U.U. Lalit and Indu Malhotra overturned that order, holding that the Travancore royal family's traditional 'shebait' (custodial) rights survive the death of the last ruler and recognising the 1949 covenant between the former Travancore-Cochin state and the Union of India as the basis for continued family involvement. Day-to-day administration today rests with an executive officer-led committee functioning alongside descendants of the royal family, with entry still governed by a traditional dress code restricted largely to Hindu worshippers. The temple remains an active centre of daily ritual worship, its annual Painkuni and Alpashy festivals drawing large numbers of pilgrims to a shrine whose unresolved Vault B keeps it as much a subject of ongoing legal and public curiosity as of devotion.
