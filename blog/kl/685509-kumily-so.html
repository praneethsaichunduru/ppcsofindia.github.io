Thekkady Wildlife-Asian Elephant

തേക്കടി വന്യജീവി - ഏഷ്യൻ ആന

The wildlife landscape around Thekkady takes its present form from an act of colonial-era engineering rather than any older legend: between 1887 and 1895, British engineer John Pennycuick oversaw construction of the Mullaperiyar Dam at the confluence of the Mullayar and Periyar rivers, built to divert water eastward through a tunnel into the Vaigai basin to irrigate arid tracts around Madurai in the then Madras Presidency. The resulting reservoir, an artificial lake of roughly 26 square kilometres set amid the Cardamom Hills, transformed a stretch of dense jungle into the scenic basin around which the sanctuary later grew, and the surrounding forest was formally declared the Periyar Lake Reserve in 1899.

Protection of the area evolved in stages over the following decades: the Maharaja of Travancore appointed S.C.H. Robinson as the first game warden, leading to the creation of the Nellikkampetty game sanctuary in 1934, which was absorbed into the state forest department in 1966 and expanded to roughly 777 square kilometres by 1950. Periyar gained its present national significance in 1978, when it was brought under Project Tiger as one of India's early tiger reserves, at a time when only a handful of tigers were confirmed in its forests; a core area of about 350 square kilometres was further notified as Periyar National Park in 1982, cementing its dual status as both a tiger reserve and a national park within the Western Ghats.

Ecologically, the reserve spans evergreen, semi-evergreen, and moist deciduous forest interspersed with grassland, supporting one of southern India's most visible wild Asian elephant (Elephas maximus) populations alongside gaur, sambar, wild pig, and a comparatively shy population of tigers that are rarely sighted directly. Its defining visitor experience, a boat cruise across the reservoir formed by the Mullaperiyar Dam, allows elephant herds and other animals to be observed as they come down to drink and bathe along the shoreline, a mode of wildlife viewing distinct from the jeep safaris typical of most Indian tiger reserves and one that depends directly on the reservoir's water level and the dam's continued operation.

A distinguishing feature of Periyar's management has been its community-based conservation model built around reformed offenders: in 2004, following the arrest of a group of wildlife poachers and sandalwood smugglers, the Kerala Forest Department organised them into the Vidiyal Vanapathukappu Sangam, retraining them as anti-poaching watchers, trekking guides, and bamboo-raft operators rather than prosecuting them conventionally. Members of this group have since taken part in patrols and intelligence-gathering that the department credits with large numbers of poaching and smuggling arrests, and the approach has been cited internationally as a model later adapted at other Indian reserves facing similar pressures from wildlife crime.

The reserve's ecodevelopment programme has since broadened to include numerous local eco-tourism committees formed around Thekkady and Kumily that run guided treks, bamboo rafting, and overnight jungle camps, channelling tourism revenue back into surrounding tribal and forest-fringe communities. The Mullaperiyar Dam itself remains the subject of a long-running inter-state water-sharing and safety dispute between Kerala and Tamil Nadu, even as it continues to be operated under Tamil Nadu's control per a colonial-era lease agreement. Periyar Tiger Reserve today is jointly shaped by these two legacies, an aging dam whose reservoir still draws the elephant herds that made Thekkady famous, and a forest department-led conservation apparatus staffed in part by the very men once arrested for poaching within its boundaries.
