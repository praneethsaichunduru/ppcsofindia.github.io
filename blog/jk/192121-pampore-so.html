Kongposh (Saffron)

کونگپوش (زعفران)

The name kesar, used across North India for saffron, derives from Sanskrit kesara, while Kashmiris call it kong or kongposh, a term that lends its name to this pictorial cancellation. The spice comes from the dried stigmas of Crocus sativus, a flower that blooms briefly each autumn across the karewa plateaus surrounding Pampore in the Pulwama-Anantnag belt of the Kashmir Valley. Local tradition traces cultivation here to a Persian or Central Asian introduction, though the twelfth-century Sanskrit chronicle Rajatarangini, compiled by the historian Kalhana, already records saffron as an established agricultural product of the valley by the eighth century CE, suggesting a continuous history of well over a thousand years.

Kashmiri saffron occupies a distinct place among the world's saffron varieties, prized for its long, deep-crimson stigmas and unusually high concentrations of crocin, picrocrocin, and safranal, the compounds responsible for its colour, bitterness, and aroma respectively. Grown at an altitude of roughly 1,600 to 1,800 metres, higher than most Iranian or Spanish saffron-growing regions, the Kashmiri strain, sometimes classified as Crocus sativus Cashmirianus, is widely regarded by agronomists as the most potent saffron grown anywhere. In 2020 the Indian government granted Kashmiri saffron a Geographical Indication tag, formally distinguishing it from cheaper, often adulterated saffron sold under its name and giving legal backing to efforts to protect its market value and authenticity.

Pampore, often called the saffron town of India, sits at the centre of this cultivation, with the karewa highlands around it accounting for the bulk of the valley's saffron acreage alongside smaller tracts in Budgam, Kishtwar, and parts of Srinagar district. The corm-propagated Crocus sativus flowers for only a short window each October and November, and each lavender bloom yields just three fragile red stigmas that must be hand-picked at dawn before the flower wilts, then separated and dried, often over charcoal or in shade, within hours of harvest. It takes an estimated 150,000 or more flowers, and many hours of delicate manual labour, to produce a single kilogram of dried saffron, which is part of why the spice remains one of the most expensive agricultural commodities by weight anywhere in the world.

The crop has, however, come under mounting strain in recent decades. Area under saffron cultivation in Kashmir fell from around 5,700 hectares in the mid-1990s to under 2,400 hectares by 2019-2020, a decline attributed to erratic rainfall, falling groundwater levels, urban and real-estate expansion onto karewa land, and competition from cheaper imported saffron. The Centre's National Saffron Mission, launched in 2010 with an outlay of roughly four hundred crore rupees to fund borewell irrigation and processing infrastructure for growers, has been widely criticised by Pampore's farmers as slow and insufficient, and several recent seasons, including sharp production slumps linked to scant winter and autumn rainfall, have left output far below historical averages of fifteen to seventeen metric tonnes a year.

A dedicated Saffron Park and the Pampore Saffron Trading Centre, developed under state and central schemes, now serve as collection and auction points where local growers can sell their produce with greater transparency on pricing, while the Sher-e-Kashmir University of Agricultural Sciences and Technology has run research stations in the area aimed at improving corm quality and irrigation efficiency. Despite these interventions, saffron farming in Pampore remains largely a smallholder, family-run activity passed down through generations, and the postal pictorial cancellation issued from Pampore SO marks this spice as one of the region's most distinctive agricultural exports, commemorated here even as the farmers who grow it contend with a steadily shrinking and increasingly uncertain harvest.
