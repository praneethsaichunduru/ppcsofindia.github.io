Unakoti

উনকোটি

The name Unakoti, drawn from Bengali and meaning literally "one less than a crore" (ten million minus one), is tied to a popular legend in which a vast company of deities travelling with Shiva to Kashi (Varanasi) halted for the night at this forested hillside in present-day Tripura. Shiva decreed that all must rise before dawn to continue the journey, but when only he awoke on time, the rest were cursed to turn to stone where they lay. The resulting host of rock-cut faces and figures, one short of a full crore by legend's count, gave the site both its name and its enduring association with failed vigilance in sacred company.

Unakoti is recognised as one of Northeast India's foremost rock-cut heritage sites and a long-standing centre of Shaivite devotion, its carvings depicting Shiva in multiple forms alongside other Hindu deities across a hillside setting in what is now Unakoti district, formed in 2012 and named directly after the site. The sculptures are counted among the largest bas-relief rock carvings in India, and the site has drawn comparisons to Cambodia's Angkor Wat for the scale of its artistic undertaking. In 2022 Unakoti was placed on UNESCO's tentative list for World Heritage status, formal recognition of a significance that scholars and the Archaeological Survey of India had already attached to it for decades.

On stylistic grounds, art historians date the carvings to roughly the 7th to 9th centuries CE, attributing their patronage to the Deva dynasty that ruled the ancient region of Samatata, with the site's active life as a pilgrimage centre extending further under later rulers including the Pala kings. The carvings were made directly into natural rock faces and boulders rather than built up as masonry, a technique that has left them exposed to centuries of weathering. The dominant image, Unakotiswara Kal Bhairava, is a colossal head of Shiva rising to roughly 30 feet including its elaborately embroidered headdress, which alone accounts for about 10 feet of that height, making it one of the largest Shiva images carved anywhere in India.

Flanking the great Shiva head are two large female figures carved into the rock, one representing Durga mounted on her lion, positioned amid the folds of the headdress alongside the central image; scattered across the surrounding hillside and stream bed are further carvings, including a prominent figure of Ganesha, along with numerous smaller reliefs of other deities half-buried in earth or moss. Unlike a single planned temple complex, Unakoti reads as an accumulation of devotional sculpture spread organically across the landscape, with carvings set into rock walls, loose boulders, and the banks of the Unakoti stream that gives the district its name.

The site today falls under the Kailashahar subdivision of Unakoti district and functions as its principal tourist and pilgrimage draw, with the Archaeological Survey of India and the state tourism department maintaining the grounds and access paths through the forest. Each year the Ashokastami Mela, held in April, draws large numbers of pilgrims to the site, with a smaller gathering also held in January, keeping the carvings in continuous ritual use rather than serving only as an archaeological curiosity. Despite its scale and the UNESCO tentative listing, Unakoti remains comparatively lightly visited next to India's better-known heritage circuits, a position local authorities are now working to change as conservation and promotion efforts proceed in tandem.
