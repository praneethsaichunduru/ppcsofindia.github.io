टाइगर एटीआर

The name Achanakmar is drawn from the forested plateau region it occupies in the Maikal range, a spur of the Satpura hills straddling the border of Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh. Long before its wildlife designation, the area was home to the Baiga, a particularly vulnerable tribal group whose traditional shifting cultivation and forest-dwelling way of life in these sal-dominated hills predates any formal conservation boundary. The sanctuary itself was first constituted in 1975 under the Wildlife Protection Act of 1972, giving legal shape to a landscape that had functioned as de facto forest commons for generations before protection formalised its limits.

Achanakmar was elevated to tiger reserve status in February 2009, becoming one of the newer additions to India's Project Tiger network and Chhattisgarh's principal stronghold for the species alongside Udanti-Sitanadi and Indravati. The reserve forms a key link in the Central Indian tiger landscape, connected to Kanha Tiger Reserve in Madhya Pradesh via the recognised Kanha-Achanakmar wildlife corridor, a connection considered important for maintaining gene flow among otherwise fragmented tiger populations. It is also nested within the larger Achanakmar-Amarkantak Biosphere Reserve, a designation that extends conservation planning beyond the reserve's own boundary into adjoining multi-use forest landscapes shared with Madhya Pradesh.

As notified in 2009, the reserve covers a total area of roughly 914 square kilometres, split between a core or critical tiger habitat of about 626 square kilometres and a surrounding buffer of around 288 square kilometres. The terrain is characterised by dense sal and mixed deciduous forest across undulating hill country, interspersed with grassy meadows and perennial streams that support a faunal assemblage including tigers, leopards, sloth bears, wild dogs, and several deer and antelope species such as sambar, chital, and the four-horned antelope. Management follows the standard Project Tiger framework, with a core zone kept free of human settlement activity and a buffer zone permitting regulated use, overseen by the Chhattisgarh forest department's dedicated tiger reserve field directorate based at Bilaspur.

A defining and often contentious feature of Achanakmar's administration has been the relocation of forest villages from its core zone. In December 2009, under Phase I of the resettlement programme, six villages, Kumha, Sambhardarha, Bankal, Jalda, Bahaur and Bokrakachhar, comprising some 245 families, were moved out of the critical tiger habitat to designated resettlement sites, with compensation packages under the National Tiger Conservation Authority's relocation scheme. Nineteen further villages remained within the core as of recent assessments, and subsequent relocation phases have drawn scrutiny from researchers and advocacy groups over delays in promised compensation and land allotment, making Achanakmar a frequently cited case study in the debate over how India implements voluntary village relocation from tiger reserves.

More recent developments at Achanakmar have included camera-trap based tiger population monitoring under the All India Tiger Estimation exercise and proposals, contested by national tiger authorities on account of core-area disturbance, for infrastructure such as optical fibre cabling through reserve land. The reserve today is administered jointly by the state forest department's field director and divisional officials headquartered in Bilaspur district, with day-to-day protection managed through a network of forest beats and anti-poaching camps staffed largely by local personnel, including members of the Baiga community still resident in buffer villages. It remains one of the smaller tiger populations among India's reserves, but its corridor role linking Chhattisgarh's forests to the larger Kanha landscape keeps it a working piece of the wider Central Indian conservation network rather than an isolated protected pocket.
