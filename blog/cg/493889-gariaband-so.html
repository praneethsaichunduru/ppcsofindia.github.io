Wild Buffalo

जंगली भैंसा

The wild water buffalo, known scientifically as Bubalus arnee, takes its species name from the Hindi and Sanskrit-derived "arna" or "arni," terms long used across the subcontinent for the untamed buffalo of river valleys and floodplain grasslands. It is the wild progenitor from which the domestic water buffalo was derived many centuries ago through selective taming, making the Gariaband population, found within the Udanti-Sitanadi Tiger Reserve, a living link to that ancestral stock rather than a feral descendant of domestic stock. The animal's association with this particular stretch of Chhattisgarh forest dates back generations, with local Gond and other tribal communities having long recognised the deep-forest buffalo herds as distinct from the cattle and buffalo kept in their villages.

Classified as Endangered on the IUCN Red List, with the global population estimated at under 4,000 individuals, Bubalus arnee is considered one of the rarest large mammals on the Indian subcontinent, and the genetically pure population surviving in Chhattisgarh is smaller still. Udanti-Sitanadi, declared the state's third tiger reserve in 2008 by merging the Udanti and Sitanadi wildlife sanctuaries, is one of only two locations in India, alongside Assam's floodplains, where an unhybridised wild lineage persists. Its formal status as the state animal of Chhattisgarh since the state's creation in 2000 gives the species an institutional profile well beyond its numbers, anchoring conservation planning, forest department messaging, and wildlife tourism branding around Gariaband district specifically.

Adult wild buffalo are considerably larger and more heavily built than domestic animals, with bulls capable of standing well over 1.5 metres at the shoulder and carrying wide, crescent-shaped horns that can span more than a metre across, among the longest of any living bovid. Population counts kept by the Chhattisgarh forest department have tracked a steep decline in Udanti-Sitanadi, from an estimated 39 to 47 animals in 2005 to a count of roughly 9 to 16 individuals in more recent surveys, with at times a single confirmed breeding female representing the entire future of the local sub-population. The principal drivers identified by wildlife biologists are habitat fragmentation, competition with free-ranging domestic livestock for forage and water, and genetic swamping through interbreeding with domestic buffalo that stray into the reserve's grasslands.

A notable response to this crisis has been the Central India Wild Buffalo Recovery Project, developed jointly by the Wildlife Institute of India, the Wildlife Trust of India, and the state forest department, which combines in-situ habitat management with an ex-situ breeding programme. In one widely reported intervention, a team of forest guards, veterinarians, and caretakers transported wild buffaloes roughly 1,900 kilometres overland from Assam's Manas National Park to Chhattisgarh to introduce fresh, unrelated genetic stock into the dwindling local gene pool, an effort aimed squarely at averting the inbreeding depression threatening the last few Udanti-Sitanadi animals.

The newly developed Barnawapara Conservation Breeding Centre, along with holding enclosures established at Pamed Wildlife Sanctuary and Indravati Tiger Reserve, now forms the operational core of this five-point recovery plan, approved by the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, under which translocated and local buffaloes are being managed in controlled conditions before any eventual release back into protected habitat. Forest officials in Gariaband continue to monitor the reserve's remaining free-ranging animals using camera traps and radio-collaring alongside this breeding effort, making the wild buffalo programme here one of the more closely watched single-species rescue operations currently under way in an Indian tiger reserve.
