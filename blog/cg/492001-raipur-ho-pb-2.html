Chhattisgarh - A "Rice Bowl"

छत्तीसगढ़ - एक "धान के कटोरा"

Chhattisgarh's standing as the "Rice Bowl" of central India rests on the vast paddy-growing plain that stretches across the Mahanadi basin, encompassing Raipur, Durg, Bilaspur, and the surrounding districts. The designation reflects both the scale of cultivation, with rice occupying roughly 3.6 million hectares of the state's agricultural land, and the antiquity of the practice, which predates the state's own formation in November 2000 by many centuries. Oral tradition and colonial-era gazetteers alike describe the region's economy as organised almost entirely around the monsoon paddy cycle, a pattern that continues to shape land use, rural livelihoods, and the rhythm of local festivals tied to sowing and harvest.

Beyond its economic weight, the title signals an ecological and genetic distinction rarely matched elsewhere in India: Chhattisgarh is recognised as one of the richest reservoirs of indigenous rice germplasm in the world. Varieties developed through generations of farmer selection include types suited to deep flooding, drought, and even distinctive medicinal or aromatic qualities, a diversity that botanists and plant breeders have drawn upon for decades. This genetic range, combined with the sheer volume of annual production, places the state among India's leading rice contributors and gives the "Rice Bowl" identity both a productive and a scientific dimension.

The systematic documentation of this diversity owes much to the agronomist Dr Radhelal Harelal Richharia, who directed the Madhya Pradesh Rice Research Institute at Raipur from 1971 to 1977 and personally organised the collection of rice germplasm from across the state during the 1970s. His surveys catalogued well over 17,000 distinct cultivars from the Chhattisgarh region alone, part of a larger body of some 19,000 accessions gathered from 42 districts, at a time when the spread of high-yielding Green Revolution varieties threatened to displace older, locally adapted strains. His work established the scientific basis for recognising Chhattisgarh not merely as a high-volume producer but as a centre of rice biodiversity worth deliberate conservation.

Among the varieties this research brought to attention are strains capable of growing under several metres of standing water, others maturing in as little as sixty days against others that take up to one hundred and fifty, and types prized for scented grain or unusually high protein content, alongside extremes such as the long-grained and tiny-grained dokra-dokri varieties. These attributes, developed without formal breeding programmes, illustrate the depth of agronomic knowledge embedded in Chhattisgarhi farming communities over successive generations. The tradition extends into local cuisine as well, where rice beer, known regionally as mahua-paired paej or landa among tribal communities, remains a staple ceremonial drink brewed from the same grain that dominates the fields.

That germplasm collection now forms the core holding of the Indira Gandhi Krishi Vishwavidyalaya in Raipur, whose rice gene bank, built substantially on Richharia's original accessions, ranks as the largest in India and among the largest in the world, with close to 23,000 varieties preserved for research and breeding purposes. State agricultural agencies continue to run seed conservation and farmer-support schemes built around this legacy, promoting both high-yield cultivation for food security and the safeguarding of traditional cultivars for future crop improvement. The university's gene bank today functions as the practical custodian of the diversity that originally earned Chhattisgarh its name, linking the state's agrarian identity to an active programme of scientific preservation rather than leaving it as a historical footnote.
