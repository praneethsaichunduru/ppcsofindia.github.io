Asiatic Cheetah

एशियाई चीता

The cheetah's name derives from the Sanskrit chitraka, meaning "the spotted one," and the animal was known across the Indian subcontinent for millennia before its disappearance from the wild. Unlike the lion or tiger, the Asiatic cheetah (Acinonyx jubatus venaticus) was prized less as a trophy of the hunt itself than as a hunting instrument: Mughal and later regional courts kept trained cheetahs for coursing blackbuck and other antelope across open grassland, a practice recorded in miniature paintings and court chronicles stretching back to the sixteenth century. Koriya, then a princely state in the Chhota Nagpur plateau region now part of Chhattisgarh, lay within the dry deciduous forest and grassland belt that formed part of the animal's historical range in central India.

The cheetah's significance for Koriya is primarily historical and ecological rather than architectural: the district is associated with the documented final stand of the species in the Indian wild. Accounts place the shooting of the last confirmed trio of wild Asiatic cheetahs, three males from a single litter, in Koriya's forests in 1947, an episode credited to the Maharaja of Surguja, Ramanuj Pratap Singh Deo, a hunter whose recorded tally of big-cat kills ran into the hundreds. A further unconfirmed sighting of a female cheetah in the same district around 1951 is often cited as the species' last trace in India before its formal declaration as extinct within the country in 1952, the first mammal species to be officially written off in independent India.

The decline that culminated in Koriya was decades in the making. Cheetahs had once ranged over much of the Indian plains, from the Deccan and Tamil Nadu in the south to the Gangetic belt and Rajasthan in the north, surviving in grassland and scrub habitats that supported the blackbuck and chinkara herds on which they preyed. Sport hunting by both colonial officials and Indian royalty, combined with the systematic conversion of grasslands into farmland and the animal's practical disappearance from any protected status, left wild populations fragmented and unsustainably small by the early twentieth century; unlike the tiger or lion, no organized sanctuary or breeding program existed for the cheetah in India before it vanished, leaving the Koriya shootings as essentially the closing chapter of an already near-complete collapse.

The episode gained fresh public attention after 2022, when India's Cheetah reintroduction project translocated African cheetahs (Acinonyx jubatus) from Namibia and South Africa to Kuno National Park in neighbouring Madhya Pradesh, reviving the species' presence in central Indian habitat for the first time since the 1950s. Because the genetically distinct Asiatic subspecies had become functionally unrecoverable, surviving today only in small numbers in Iran, the reintroduction relied on the African cheetah as an ecological substitute, a choice that drew scientific debate but also returned attention to historical source regions such as Koriya, whose terrain and former cheetah population are frequently invoked in discussions of the species' original Indian range.

Present-day Koriya, now a district within Chhattisgarh and administratively separate from the former Surguja princely territory, retains tracts of forest around the Guru Ghasidas (Sanjay) National Park landscape that once supported cheetah habitat, though no wild cheetah population has existed there for more than seven decades. The 1947 shootings are documented largely through surviving photographs and princely-era hunting records rather than any on-site memorial, and the district's forest department today manages the area chiefly for its tiger and biodiversity conservation value under the wider Chhattisgarh forest administration. For philatelic and postal purposes, the Baikunthapur post office's association with the Asiatic cheetah functions as a marker of this specific historical footnote, commemorating a place tied to the documented end of the species in the Indian wild rather than to any living population or protected reserve.
