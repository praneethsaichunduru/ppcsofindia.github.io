Semarsot Sanctuary: Spotted Deer, Semarsot River

सेमरसोत अभयारण्य: चित्तीदार हिरण, सेमरसोत नदी

The name Semarsot derives from the small settlement of the same name that sits along the Ambikapur-Daltonganj road in the Surguja region, roughly fifty kilometres from Ambikapur town, and it was from this locality that the surrounding forest tract took its identity when it was constituted as a protected area. Unlike many Indian sanctuaries tied to a royal hunting ground or a princely decree, Semarsot's designation reflects a more administrative impulse of the late twentieth century: the recognition that the sal and mixed deciduous forests straddling the Surguja plateau, drained by the Semarsot river and its tributary streams, merited formal protection as pressure on the region's forest cover increased.

Semarsot Wildlife Sanctuary was notified in 1978 and today extends across approximately 430 square kilometres, making it one of the larger protected areas in northern Chhattisgarh though far less visited than the state's tiger reserves at Achanakmar or Udanti-Sitanadi. Its significance lies chiefly in its ecological role: the sanctuary shelters spotted deer (chital) alongside sambar, nilgai, chinkara, wild boar, fox and jungle cat, and its forests of sal mixed with riverine tree cover along the Semarsot valley form part of a broader habitat mosaic that sustains both resident herbivore populations and the predators that depend on them.

The sanctuary's terrain is characterised by moist and dry tropical deciduous forest typical of the Central Indian highlands, with the Semarsot river and associated nullahs supplying perennial and seasonal water that shapes the distribution of grazing grounds used heavily by chital herds. No single architect or ruler is credited with the sanctuary's creation; rather, its boundaries were drawn by the state forest department to consolidate reserve forest tracts already under working-plan management, and its administration has since rested with the Chhattisgarh forest department's wildlife wing, which oversees anti-poaching patrols, water-hole maintenance and controlled grazing within the notified area.

A distinguishing feature of Semarsot is its position within a larger conservation corridor: together with the neighbouring Tamor Pingla Wildlife Sanctuary and Guru Ghasidas National Park, it forms part of a connected forest belt along the Chhattisgarh-Jharkhand-Madhya Pradesh tri-junction that wildlife biologists have identified as a movement corridor linking the Bandhavgarh Tiger Reserve in Madhya Pradesh with the Palamau Tiger Reserve in Jharkhand, giving the sanctuary a conservation value well beyond its own modest size.

Administratively, Semarsot now falls within Balrampur-Ramanujganj district, carved out of the larger Surguja district in 2012, and its management continues under that district's forest division as part of the state's wider push to strengthen the Guru Ghasidas-Tamor Pingla tiger landscape through corridor restoration and anti-poaching coordination with adjoining states. The sanctuary draws far fewer visitors than Chhattisgarh's marquee reserves, functioning instead as a quietly managed buffer of sal and riverine forest whose chital populations and water resources remain as important to the Surguja plateau's ecology as they are to the tribal communities who continue to depend on its forest produce for their livelihoods.
