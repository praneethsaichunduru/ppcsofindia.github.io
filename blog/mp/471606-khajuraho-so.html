Khajuraho Temple

खजुराहो को मंदिर

The name Khajuraho is popularly traced to the Hindi word khajur, meaning date palm, with tradition holding that golden date-palm trees once flanked the gates of the ancient town; some accounts also link the name to Khajuravahaka, an earlier designation recorded in regional sources. The site served as a religious and cultural centre of the Chandela dynasty, which rose from feudatory status under the Pratiharas in the ninth century to become an independent power in the Jejakabhukti region of central India by the early tenth century, reaching its height under rulers such as Yashovarman and Dhanga. Inscriptions left by successive Chandela kings name individual patrons and help date most of the principal shrines, giving Khajuraho an unusually firm historical record compared with many other medieval Indian temple sites.

Khajuraho is counted among India's foremost examples of temple architecture and devotional sculpture, with its significance resting less on any single structure than on the scale and artistic unity of the complex as a whole. The temples served Hindu and Jain worship simultaneously, with Chandela patronage extending across sectarian lines; the Lakshmana temple, built under Yashovarman around 954 CE, honours Vishnu, while the Vishvanatha and Parshvanatha temples, associated with Dhanga, represent Shaiva and Jain devotion respectively. This plural religious character, combined with the density and quality of the surviving sculpture, has made Khajuraho a frequent subject of art-historical study and placed it among the most visited heritage sites in Madhya Pradesh.

The temples were raised chiefly in buff, pink, and pale yellow sandstone over roughly a single century, from the mid-tenth to mid-eleventh century, employing the Nagara mode of northern Indian temple design, in which the superstructure rises as a curvilinear shikhara built up from repeating miniature spires. The Kandariya Mahadeva temple, commissioned under the ruler Vidyadhara in the early eleventh century, exemplifies this at its most developed: it measures roughly 31 metres long and 20 metres wide, with its main tower reaching about 31 metres in height and composed of eighty-four subsidiary spirelets clustered around the central shikhara. Its walls alone carry close to 800 carved figures, ranging from deities and celestial attendants to the erotic couples for which the site is best known internationally.

Among the three clusters into which the surviving temples are grouped, the western group holds the largest and most elaborate shrines, including the Kandariya Mahadeva, Lakshmana, and Vishvanatha temples, and draws the bulk of visitor attention; the eastern and southern groups, more scattered and comparatively plain, include several Jain temples such as the Parshvanatha, notable for sculptural friezes that are religious rather than erotic in subject. The erotic carvings that distinguish the western group have drawn varied scholarly explanations, from tantric symbolism to rites of passage and apotropaic protection of the temple against lightning and other calamities, and they remain the single most discussed and debated feature of the site in both academic and popular writing.

The Khajuraho group is protected and maintained by the Archaeological Survey of India, which oversees conservation of the stone fabric and the grounds, and the site has been inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1986 under the designation Khajuraho Group of Monuments. A dedicated Archaeological Museum adjacent to the western group displays sculptures recovered from the wider area, supplementing what remains in situ, and the annual Khajuraho Dance Festival stages classical Indian dance performances against the backdrop of the illuminated temples each year. Today the complex functions as both an active pilgrimage destination for the Shaiva, Vaishnava, and Jain shrines still in worship and a major stop on India's heritage tourism circuit, administered jointly by the ASI and the Madhya Pradesh tourism department.
