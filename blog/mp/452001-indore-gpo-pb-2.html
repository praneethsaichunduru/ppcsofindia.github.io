Krishnapura Chhatri

कृष्णपुरा छत्री

The Krishnapura Chhatris take their name from Krishna Bai Holkar, wife of the Maratha general Yashwantrao Holkar I and mother of Malhar Rao Holkar II, in whose memory the principal cenotaph complex was raised on the banks of the Khan river in Indore. A chhatri, literally an umbrella-shaped memorial canopy, is the customary form used across Rajasthan and Central India to mark the site where a person of rank was cremated, since Hindu practice does not inter the dead in a grave to be marked by a tomb. The Krishnapura site grew over time into a cluster commemorating several Holkar rulers rather than a single individual, giving the complex its present character as a dynastic memorial ground rather than one isolated monument.

As a funerary monument of the Holkar dynasty, which governed the princely state of Indore from the early eighteenth century until its accession to independent India in 1948, Krishnapura Chhatri ranks among the city's principal heritage sites tied directly to its ruling house, alongside the nearby Rajwada palace roughly half a kilometre away. The Holkars, who rose from a Maratha military clan to become one of the most powerful courts of the Maratha confederacy, used such chhatris both as acts of filial and dynastic piety and as public statements of the family's status, wealth, and continuity, making the site historically significant less for any single religious function than for what it records about Holkar succession and patronage over nearly two centuries.

The complex as it survives today is understood to have taken its present form through construction and additions carried out over the nineteenth century, with the principal structure largely completed by the mid-1800s and a prominent later cenotaph added for Tukoji Rao Holkar II. Built of carved stone in a blend of Maratha, Rajput, and Mughal idioms, the ensemble comprises three main chhatris and five smaller cenotaphs set within a single precinct, along with a stepped water tank, five subsidiary temples, a samadhi, and a deepmaal (lamp tower), reflecting the layered way the site expanded as successive rulers and family members were commemorated there rather than being built to a single original plan.

A distinguishing feature of the complex is its visual and spatial relationship to the Khan river, with a flight of steps descending from the rear of the main chhatri directly to the riverbank, linking the memorial precinct to the water in the manner favored for royal cenotaphs across the region. The carved spires, arched pavilions, and jaali lattice screens of the main structure are frequently cited as among the finer surviving examples of nineteenth-century Maratha decorative stonework in Indore, distinguishing Krishnapura from the plainer civic architecture that surrounds it in the older quarters of the city near the Sarafa market.

Encroachment, pollution of the adjoining Khan river, and years of civic neglect left parts of the complex deteriorated by the early twenty-first century, prompting cleanup and conservation efforts from around 2018 involving the Indore Municipal Corporation alongside state heritage bodies. The site today functions as both a protected heritage monument and a local landmark within walking distance of Rajwada, drawn into municipal river-rejuvenation and heritage-walk initiatives that have brought it renewed public attention even as its carved stonework continues to show the strain of its riverside setting.
