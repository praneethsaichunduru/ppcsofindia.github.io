Chhau Dance

छऊ नाच

The term Chhau is generally traced to the Odia word chhauni, meaning a military camp or barracks, reflecting the dance form's roots in the martial drills, or paika akhdas, once practised by the foot soldiers of regional feudal estates across the Chota Nagpur plateau and the Mayurbhanj and Purulia tracts. In Seraikella, then a princely state under the Singh Deo dynasty, these martial exercises were gradually stylised into a courtly performance art from the nineteenth century onward, as successive rulers took a direct and unusually hands-on interest in shaping it. Unlike patrons elsewhere, members of the Seraikella royal family did not merely sponsor the dance but personally choreographed and performed it, a practice credited with transforming raw paika movement into the more lyrical, stylised form recognised today.

As one of three principal schools of Chhau, alongside the Mayurbhanj style of Odisha and the Purulia style of West Bengal, Seraikella Chhau occupies a distinct place as the only variant historically cultivated inside a royal court rather than solely among village and tribal troupes. It is classed as a semi-classical dance-drama that fuses martial footwork, acrobatic leaps, and mime with themes drawn from Hindu epics, regional folklore, and depictions of nature such as birds, hunters, and seasonal moods. Its significance was given international standing in November 2010, when UNESCO inscribed Chhau, encompassing all three regional styles, on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, citing its rare fusion of martial, tribal, and classical performance vocabularies.

Central to Seraikella's distinct identity is its mask-making tradition, credited in large part to Kumar Bijoy Pratap Singh Deo, a member of the royal family who is widely regarded as having refined both the choreography and the mask craft in the early twentieth century and who helped introduce the form to audiences in Europe during the 1930s. The masks themselves are built up in layers of linen and papier-mache over clay moulds, finished with a smooth coat of fine clay drawn traditionally from the bed of the nearby Kharkai river, and then painted in soft pastel tones with delicate brushwork to render serene, large-eyed faces representing gods, demons, animals, and human archetypes. Because these masks cover the performer's face completely, dancers must convey the full emotional register of a character, from valour to longing, through the body, neck, and footwork alone, a discipline that demands years of rigorous training distinct from unmasked Indian classical forms.

The dance's principal public stage remains the Chaitra Parva festival, held each spring around Basant Panchami and traditionally dedicated to Ardhanareeswara, the composite half-male, half-female form of Shiva, as a ritual invocation for a good harvest. In Seraikella town the festival is staged in three overlapping settings in the same season, a modest courtyard performance within the old palace grounds, a larger government-organised event at the town stadium, and a temple-ground performance near the local Shiva shrine, together drawing troupes that also perform guest items in the Mayurbhanj and Purulia styles. Mask-makers for these performances are drawn from several tribal and caste communities local to the Seraikella-Kharsawan area, sustaining a craft economy tightly bound to the dance's annual performance calendar.

Training today continues chiefly through government-backed institutions established in the town, including the Rajkiya Chhau Nritya Kala Kendra, a state-run academy that has for decades trained successive generations of dancers under senior gurus, alongside smaller private centres such as the Trinetra Chhau Dance Centre, associated with the Padma Shri awardee Gopal Prasad Dubey. These institutions, working alongside Jharkhand's state cultural affairs department, have kept Seraikella Chhau active as a living, taught discipline rather than a historical curiosity, with students drawn from the local Singh Deo-era performing families as well as newer entrants from across the district. The dance's continued presence on this postal cancellation reflects its standing as the defining cultural marker of Seraikella-Kharsawan district, a form still rehearsed in the same courtyard where its royal patrons once refined it.
